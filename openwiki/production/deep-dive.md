# **Comparative Structural Analysis of Global Coffee Supply Chains: Economic Architecture, Institutional Dynamics, and Value Distribution Across Six Major Origins**

## **Executive Summary**

The global coffee value chain represents a complex economic landscape characterized by sharp structural contrasts between high-volume commodity streams and high-value specialty sectors. This report presents an economic and institutional analysis of coffee production and export architectures across six pivotal origins: Colombia, Peru, Ethiopia, Kenya, Rwanda, and Indonesia. These origins embody diverse models of land tenure, primary post-harvest processing, institutional governance, and market power distribution.  
The structural matrix of coffee origin economics ranges from highly centralized state-regulated models to fragmented, market-driven intermediary networks. In origins like Kenya and Rwanda, post-harvest infrastructure has historically relied on centralized Coffee Washing Stations (CWS) or cooperative factories, governed by state regulatory bodies such as Kenya's Capital Markets Authority (CMA) and Nairobi Coffee Exchange (NCE), and Rwanda’s National Agricultural Export Development Board (NAEB)[^1]. Conversely, Colombia, Peru, and Indonesia are predominantly characterized by decentralized, on-farm primary processing (*beneficios húmedos* or micro-milling), leaving dry milling (*trilladoras*) and secondary consolidation as the primary loci of structural power[^5].  
Recent legislative reforms have altered historical trading pathways. Ethiopia's Directive No. 433/2019 dismantled the mandatory requirement for export coffee to pass through the Ethiopia Commodity Exchange (ECX), enabling direct export pathways through vertical integration[^9]. Kenya’s implementation of the Direct Settlement System (DSS) under the Coffee Act reforms has sought to bypass predatory marketing agents by automating direct, rapid fund disbursements to farmer accounts[^2]. Meanwhile, Rwanda’s 2023 repeal of its Coffee Zoning Policy unleashed free-market competition for raw cherries, driving up farmgate prices while simultaneously disrupting established CWS supply partnerships and plot-level traceability structures[^3].  
The specialty coffee sector—defined by cup scores of 80+ points, single-estate or single-washing-station provenance, and specialized processing—acts as a disruptive force against conventional commodity channels. Specialty value chains reconfigure traditional supply networks by establishing direct trade protocols, offering quality-tier price premiums that bypass conventional middlemen, and demanding granular plot-level polygon mapping to comply with international regulations such as the European Union Deforestation Regulation (EUDR)[^3]. However, the equitable distribution of Free on Board (FOB) price export margins remains constrained by structural bottlenecks, including regional dry mill monopolies, localized credit-lock mechanisms by informal buyers (*acopiadores*, *tengkulak*), and the administrative overhead of cooperative management[^5]. Practitioner accounts increasingly caution against a caricature of intermediaries, however: middlemen and importers perform genuine aggregation, quality-control, and trade-financing functions, and many "direct trade" claims overstate actual disintermediation[^25].

## **Origin-by-Origin Deep Dives**

> **Data note:** The "Share of FOB Export Price" tables in this report are directional estimates synthesized from practitioner accounts (green-buyer, exporter, and producer interviews) and trade literature; they are not audited national statistics and should be read as indicative ranges only.

### **Colombia**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Colombia's coffee landscape is dominated by smallholder coffee farming. Approximately 95% of the nation’s estimated 500,000–560,000 coffee-producing families[^37] cultivate plots smaller than 5 hectares, with an overall national average plot size ranging between 1.5 and 2.0 hectares. Medium-sized estates (5 to 20 hectares) account for roughly 4% of production, while large commercial plantations exceeding 20 hectares represent less than 1% of total coffee land area.  
Land ownership is structured around private individual land titles (*escrituras públicas*), backed by national property registries. Ancestral and customary tenure arrangements exist primarily within indigenous reserves (*resguardos indígenas*) located in regions such as Cauca, Nariño, and Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, where land is collectively managed by local councils (*cabildos*).  
Labor dynamics present a fundamental operational challenge to Colombian coffee production. Smallholder operations rely predominantly on unpaid family labor. Medium and large estates rely on seasonal migratory labor (*recolectores*), who move across coffee belts following peak harvesting periods (*cosecha principal* and *mitaca*). Labor availability has contracted due to youth rural-to-urban migration and rising competition from non-agricultural sectors. Agricultural labor costs are tied to the national minimum wage and piece-rate payments per *arroba* (12.5 kg) of harvested cherry. This inflates total production costs, making labor represent up to 60–65% of total farmgate operational expenditures.

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary post-harvest processing in Colombia occurs almost entirely on-farm through decentralized wet-milling infrastructure known as *beneficios húmedos*. Smallholders utilize mechanical micro-pulpers (*despulpadoras*) and small concrete tanks for mucilage fermentation, followed by sun-drying on raised wooden roofs (*paseras* or *elvas*) or parabolic solar dryers. Centralized Coffee Washing Stations (CWS) are rare in Colombia due to rugged topography and established farm-level processing infrastructure. Producers dry their coffee until it reaches a stable moisture content of 10–12%, producing dry parchment coffee (*café pergamino seco*).  
Dry milling infrastructure (*trilladoras*) is heavily centralized and controlled by two major institutional groups: the *Federación Nacional de Cafeteros* (FNC) through its warehousing arm (*Almacafé*), and large private international and domestic export corporations (such as SKN Caribecafe, Carcafé/Volcafe, and Neumann Kaffee Gruppe). Parchment coffee is transported from farmgate to municipal purchasing points, where it undergoes sample grading (*factor de rendimiento*) to determine the yield of exportable green coffee (*café excelso*). Purchases are conventionally priced per *carga* of 125 kg of dry parchment at the FNC's yield factor 94 ("FR 94"), i.e., a carga of parchment is expected to yield roughly 94 kg of excelso green. Power dynamics at the dry mill node favor large exporters who control industrial sizing screens, gravity tables, optical sorters, and container loading logistics at main port terminals (Buenaventura, Cartagena, Santa Marta).

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

The chain of custody in Colombia moves from the farmgate seller directly to two main channels: local private buyers (*acopiadores* or *compradores de café*) or municipal cooperative buying stations (*cooperativas de caficultores*). Local *acopiadores* leverage cash liquidity to purchase parchment directly from farmers, capturing margins by blending lots and exploiting smallholders' immediate cashflow constraints. The parchment is subsequently sold to regional dry mills (*trilladoras*) for final grading and export clearance at the port.  
The institutional framework is governed by the FNC. The FNC operates the *Precio de Garantía* (guaranteed base price), a daily internal base purchase price calculated using a formula based on the New York ICE C-Contract settlement price, the USD/COP exchange rate, and the prevailing Colombian quality differential premium. The FNC's 33 regional cooperatives guarantee to purchase parchment coffee from any registered grower at this transparent price benchmark, effectively setting a market floor that private buyers must match or exceed. In practice, that floor has not always protected growers: in 2018 the FNC price sat below typical production costs, and in 2024 a producer selling through the FNC stood to profit roughly $0.35/lb versus about $0.53/lb through a private exporter; the FNC is itself funded by a 6% levy on coffee exports (the *contribución cafetera*)[^26].  
The breakdown of export value distribution across the Colombian coffee node is estimated as follows:

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Seller (Parchment)** | 75% – 82% |
| **Local Intermediary / Acopiador** | 3% – 5% |
| **Cooperative / Dry Mill Processing** | 6% – 9% |
| **Exporter & Port Logistics** | 4% – 6% |

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

The Colombian specialty coffee sector (80+ cupping scores, single-farm micro-lots, exotic varietals such as Gesha, Sidra, and Pink Bourbon) bypasses standard cooperative aggregation models. Specialty streams eliminate lot-blending at municipal buying stations by establishing direct relationships between roasters, specialized green coffee exporters (e.g., Caravela Coffee, Banexport), and individual producers.  
Direct trade relationships are operationalized legally within FNC export regulations. While the FNC historically held a virtual export monopoly, private firms and individual producers can register as exporters provided they comply with the national export register, obtain sanitary certifications (INVIMA), and pay the mandatory national coffee contribution tax (*contribución cafetera*) per pound exported. In direct-trade contracts, either the specialized exporter or the producer association holds the active export license. The registration burden is nonetheless steep enough that direct producer-level exports remain the exception; even small importers describe the process as "almost impossible" without falling back on the FNC's export services[^33].  
Traceability in Colombia is facilitated by the FNC’s *Sistema de Información Cafetera* (SICA) database, which contains polygon mapping and structural data for nearly every coffee farm nationwide. This system positions Colombia well to comply with European Union Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) mandates, allowing specialty premiums to flow directly to individual growers based on documented lot-level quality and plot-level geographic verification.

### **Peru**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Peru’s coffee production is managed by an estimated 220,000 smallholder families, with over 85% of total acreage cultivated on farms smaller than 5 hectares (averaging 2.0 to 3.0 hectares). Medium-sized farms (5 to 15 hectares) make up approximately 12% of the landscape, while commercial plantations exceeding 15 hectares represent a negligible fraction of national output.  
Land ownership models consist of formal private land titles (*títulos de propiedad*) managed by the national land agency (SUNARP), alongside customary land possession arrangements (*certificados de posesión*) issued by local municipal authorities or communal peasant organizations (*comunidades campesinas*). In indigenous Amazonian territories across major growing regions such as San Martín, Junín, Amazonas, and Cajamarca, coffee is cultivated within communal land boundaries.  
Labor reliance leans heavily on unpaid family labor during routine maintenance and early harvesting. During the peak harvest season (*zabra*), smallholders hire seasonal migratory workers (*peones*) from neighboring Andean highland regions. Labor shortages during peak harvest months have become structural due to youth out-migration and regional wage competition. Labor costs are negotiated on a daily wage (*jornal*) or per-basket (*lata*) basis. Harvest labor accounts for roughly 50–60% of total operational costs, placing financial strain on unorganized smallholders who lack operational credit.

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary post-harvest processing in Peru is decentralized and occurs almost exclusively on-farm via individual micro-mills (*beneficios húmedos*). Smallholders use manual or motorized pulpers to process freshly picked cherries, ferment the pulped coffee in small wooden or concrete tanks, and dry the wet parchment on raised drying beds or plastic-covered solar domes (*módulos de secado solar*). On-farm processing results in variable quality, as smallholders frequently market coffee as wet or semi-dry parchment (*pergamino húmedo*) due to drying capacity limitations in humid rain-forest climates. Centralized CWS are virtually non-existent in Peru due to difficult logistics and geographical dispersion.  
Dry milling infrastructure (*trilladoras*) is concentrated in strategic regional transport hubs such as Jaén (Cajamarca), Pichanaki (Junín), Chiclayo, and the port region of Callao/Lima. These dry mills are owned either by private export conglomerates (e.g., Perhusa, Olam, Louis Dreyfus Company) or by primary producer federations and agricultural cooperatives (such as NORANDINO, APROCAFE, and COOPBAM)[^6]. Parchment coffee is collected in interior towns, transported down the Andean passes to coastal dry mills, and milled, screened, optical-sorted, and bagged for export[^6].

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

The Peruvian chain of custody features a divide between private intermediary channels and cooperative pathways[^16]. In the private channel, independent collectors (*acopiadores* or *compradores local*) buy wet or dry parchment at the farmgate or in rural weekly markets[^5]. These intermediaries utilize immediate cash offers and local transport access to purchase coffee from unorganized farmers, often misgrading moisture levels to extract higher margins[^5]. The aggregated parchment is then sold to larger regional traders (*mayoristas*) who deliver to private dry mills owned by export conglomerates before final export through the Port of Callao[^6]. Alternatively, organized farmers deliver parchment directly to primary agricultural cooperatives, which process the coffee through cooperative-owned dry mills and export federations[^6].  
Peru's institutional landscape lacks a central state price-stabilization body comparable to Colombia’s FNC. Instead, agricultural cooperatives serve as the structural backbone for market access and quality certification[^16][^18]. Peru has become a leading exporter of Fairtrade and Organic certified Arabica coffee[^16][^17]. Cooperatives capture economies of scale, offer pre-harvest credit, and return certification premiums directly to members[^16]. The cooperative record is uneven, however: long-time residents of growing regions describe many cooperatives as "cooperatives in name only" that operate as private buyers and resellers, and organic certification is widely reported to be gamed (certifying a fraction of members' hectares while marketing far more volume as certified)[^35].  
The estimated value distribution across the Peruvian value chain nodes is structured as follows:

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Seller (Parchment)** | 60% – 70% |
| **Local Collector / Acopiador** | 5% – 8% |
| **Regional Trader / Cooperative Operations** | 8% – 12% |
| **Dry Mill & Export Conglomerate** | 10% – 12% |

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

The Peruvian specialty coffee sector (80+ scoring coffees, high-altitude micro-lots from regions like Villa Rica, Cusco, and Jaén) operates by disintermediating the traditional *acopiador* networks[^5]. Specialty buyers partner directly with primary cooperatives (such as Santuario) or independent specialty exporters (e.g., Red Fox Coffee Merchants) that maintain local cupping laboratories and field staff[^5].  
Direct trade agreements are operationalized under Peruvian commercial law by specialty exporters or certified cooperatives that hold active export credentials (*registro de exportador*) with the Ministry of Agricultural Development and Irrigation (MIDAGRI) and SUNAT (customs)[^6]. In direct trade configurations, the co-op or specialized exporter acts as the legal exporter of record, handling dry milling, phytosanitary clearance, and port logistics, while passing quality premiums directly to smallholders based on cupping scores[^5].  
Traceability to the single-farm level faces structural bottlenecks in Peru. Fragmented land ownership, informal customary land tenure, and remote geography impede plot-level polygon mapping necessary for EUDR compliance. Cooperatives with external technical assistance are better positioned to complete farm mapping, whereas unorganized smallholders selling to *acopiadores* risk exclusion from European supply chains due to lack of verifiable plot traceability.

### **Ethiopia**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Ethiopia’s coffee sector is anchored by an estimated 2.2 million smallholder farming households, which account for more than 95% of national coffee output. Smallholder operations are characterized by micro-plots averaging less than 0.9 hectares, categorized into four systems: forest coffee, semi-forest coffee, garden coffee, and plantation coffee. State-owned or private commercial plantations represent less than 5% of total production volume.  
Under the Ethiopian constitutional framework, land is the public property of the state and the Ethiopian people; private fee-simple land ownership does not exist. Farmers hold hereditary land-use rights (*holding certificates*) granted by regional state governments. These rights permit land cultivation and inheritance but prohibit land sale or mortgaging.  
Labor dynamics depend on family labor networks. In garden and semi-forest systems, routine farm management and selective picking are conducted by family members, supplemented during harvest by reciprocal labor-sharing arrangements (*Dabo*) among neighboring households. Commercial plantations and larger outgrower schemes hire seasonal migratory laborers from neighboring regions. Labor costs are paid as daily wages or on a per-kilo cherry picking basis. Because coffee production is deeply integrated into agroforestry garden systems, explicit labor costs are often absorbed into household subsistence economies, though rising living costs are putting upward pressure on seasonal harvest wages.

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary post-harvest processing in Ethiopia follows two traditional pathways: washed processing and sun-dried natural processing. Washed coffee accounts for roughly 30–40% of production and is processed at centralized wet mills / Coffee Washing Stations (CWS) operated by private suppliers or Primary Farmers’ Cooperative Unions (e.g., Yirgacheffe, Sidama, Oromia Cooperative Unions)[^10]. Smallholders harvest fresh cherries and transport them on foot or via pack animals to these centralized stations. Natural processed coffee (60–70% of output) is dried on raised bamboo beds (*jenfel*) — on-farm and at neighborhood drying grounds for the smallest growers, though most export naturals are processed at centralized stations that run washed and natural streams side by side[^10][^27].  
Dry milling infrastructure—comprising hulling, destoning, grading, optical sorting, and bagging facilities—is concentrated in regional coffee hubs (such as Jimma, Agaro, Hawassa, Dilla) and central industrial zones in Addis Ababa[^10]. Historically, all green coffee parchment and dried cherry had to be transported to central warehouses in Addis Ababa for mandatory government inspection and grading[^9]. Dry mills are owned by private exporters, primary cooperative unions, and state-backed agricultural enterprises.

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

The institutional framework of Ethiopian coffee trading underwent a shift with the passage of **Directive No. 433/2019** by the Council of Ministers, overseen by the Ethiopian Coffee and Tea Authority (ECTA)[^9]. Prior to 2019, the Ethiopia Commodity Exchange (ECX)—established in 2008—mandated that all commercial coffee be sold through its centralized auction floor[^10]. While the ECX provided organized grading, weighing, and payment systems, it disrupted value chain traceability by commingling coffee into standardized regional grades (e.g., Sidamo Grade 2, Yirgacheffe Grade 1), preventing specialty roasters from tracing coffee back to specific washing stations or farms[^10]. (ECX reforms have since begun publishing pre-trade lot information by region and process, but the standard auction channel still carries no farm-level geolocation[^27].)  
Directive No. 433/2019 introduced direct export pathways through vertical integration[^9]. Under this framework, registered coffee suppliers, washing station owners, and cooperative unions are legally permitted to bypass the ECX trading floor and sell coffee directly to international buyers and exporters[^9]. In the vertically integrated stream, smallholder cherries delivered to licensed suppliers or cooperative unions flow directly to the exporter and onto FOB Djibouti, bypassing the commodity floor entirely[^9]. This reform allows exporters to provide advance working capital directly to local suppliers, shortening the supply chain and preserving lot-level origin identity[^10]. Export-eligible grades 1 to 4 traded via vertical integration pathways have achieved price premiums of up to 22% over base ECX floor prices (a single-study estimate)[^9][^10]. Uptake among the smallest growers has nonetheless been limited: most smallholders lacked the credit lines, processing capacity, and payment terms to market coffee directly, so the majority continued to sell cherry through traditional channels[^27].  
The value distribution across the vertically integrated Ethiopian coffee node is outlined below:

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Cherry Seller** | 55% – 65% |
| **Primary Processor / CWS Supplier** | 12% – 18% |
| **Dry Milling & Freight (Addis to Djibouti)** | 10% – 14% |
| **Exporter Margin (Port Djibouti)** | 8% – 12% |

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

The introduction of Directive No. 433/2019 altered the specialty coffee landscape in Ethiopia[^9]. Specialty coffees (scoring 80 to 90+ points from regions like Guji, Yirgacheffe, Sidama, and West Arsi) can now bypass commodity blending and maintain plot or washing-station provenance[^10].  
Direct trade agreements are operationalized through licensed Ethiopian exporters or Primary Cooperative Unions holding direct export permits issued by the ECTA[^9]. International roasters contract directly with these exporters, specifying exact cupping profiles, preparation standards (e.g., Grade 1 zero-defect hand sorting), and washing station locations[^10].  
Traceability to the single washing station or cooperative level is established under vertical integration[^9]. However, achieving plot-level farm mapping for EUDR compliance presents administrative and logistical hurdles in Ethiopia. Because millions of smallholders cultivate micro-plots integrated into dense agroforestry garden and forest environments, polygon mapping requires immense technical resources. Unorganized smallholders outside cooperative union networks face potential risks of supply chain exclusion if plot-level geographic verification cannot be established prior to EUDR enforcement deadlines.

### **Kenya**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Kenya’s coffee sector exhibits a dual ownership structure split between smallholder farmers and estate enterprises[^19]. Smallholders account for approximately 70% of total national coffee production and are organized into Farmers Cooperative Societies (FCS)[^1]. Smallholder plots are small, averaging under 1.0 hectare (frequently containing 200–300 coffee trees per household)[^1]. The remaining 30% of national production originates from the estate sector, which comprises medium-sized estates (5 to 20 hectares) and large commercial plantations (\>20 to several hundred hectares) historically managed by private corporations or individual growers (e.g., Gethumbwini, Sasini)[^1].  
Land ownership is underpinned by formal private land titles (*title deeds*) issued under the national land registration system. Inheritance practices have resulted in progressive land fragmentation among smallholders, reducing average plot sizes over successive generations[^12].  
Labor reliance varies by production sector. FCS smallholders depend primarily on household family labor, supplementing with daily-paid local labor during peak picking periods[^1]. Estates rely on casual seasonal labor forces recruited from neighboring communities for selective cherry harvesting. Labor costs are governed by statutory agricultural minimum wage regulations and piece-rate payments per *debe* (standard measurement bucket) of harvested cherry. Labor accounts for over 50% of estate production costs. Regional labor shortages during peak harvest months have accelerated production costs across key growing regions in Nyeri, Kirinyaga, Murang'a, and Kiambu[^11].

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary post-harvest processing in Kenya is centralized for smallholders and decentralized for estates[^19]. Smallholders pick ripe cherries and deliver them on the same day to centralized washing factories (*wet mills*) managed by their respective Farmers Cooperative Society[^2]. These factories utilize multi-disc pulpers, double-fermentation washed processing systems, channel sorting, and drying on raised wooden beds (panya beds) to produce parchment coffee[^1]. Estates operate their own on-farm wet mills[^1].  
Milling infrastructure (*dry mills*) has undergone statutory restructuring following the enactment of the Coffee Act reforms (2023–2026)[^1]. Historically, commercial milling was dominated by private multinational corporations[^19]. Under recent legislative mandates aimed at curbing anti-competitive behavior — principally a separation-of-roles rule barring a single firm from acting as exporter, broker, and miller simultaneously — milling has shifted toward county-owned dry mills (e.g., Kirinyaga, Kipkelion) and the restructured KPCU, with some private mills closing in a transition practitioners describe as abrupt[^19][^30]. Dry mills process clean parchment into standardized commercial sizes: AA (screen 17/18), AB (screen 15/16), PB (Peaberry), C, TT, T, and E1.

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

The Kenyan coffee trading framework operates through an institutional architecture regulated by the Capital Markets Authority (CMA) and the Coffee Directorate[^1]. The primary price-discovery mechanism is the weekly public auction held at the Nairobi Coffee Exchange (NCE)[^1].  
In the primary supply stream, FCS smallholders deliver cherries to cooperative factories, which send processed parchment to county or public dry mills[^2]. From the mill, CMA-licensed independent coffee brokers place the graded coffee onto the NCE auction floor[^1]. Licensed export buyers bid on lots and transfer funds through a commercial clearing bank operating the Direct Settlement System (DSS), which directly deposits disbursements into the primary accounts of cooperatives and individual farmers[^2].  
Under market reforms introduced between 2023 and 2026, traditional commercial marketing agents were replaced by licensed brokers to address historical conflicts of interest — though veteran buyers note the change was partly a re-badging of the same licensed role, with broker fees on the order of 1–2% of sale value[^2][^31]. They were replaced by CMA-licensed independent coffee brokers, including county-based cooperative brokers and specialized brokerage firms, who prepare auction catalogues and represent producers on the NCE trading floor[^1].  
The **Direct Settlement System (DSS)**, operated through an appointed financial clearing institution (initially Co-operative Bank, with Stanbic Bank Kenya approved as an additional DSS provider in 2026), mandates that proceeds from NCE auction sales or direct sales be deposited into a central clearing account and disbursed directly to cooperative societies, mills, brokers, and individual farmer bank accounts — statutorily within days of exchange settlement[^13][^2]. At the exchange level this automates settlement, but practitioner accounts from 2024 note that farmgate timing still lags: FCS payouts are typically pooled and averaged after the selling season (often arriving months after harvest), and first-payment (cherry advance) systems were not yet functioning, leaving growers reliant on informal credit[^30]. The Coffee Act 2023 (enacted March 2026) caps FCS administrative and operational deductions at 10% of gross sales value — up from the 5% ceiling discussed during the 2022 reform debate — placing a statutory limit on the charges societies may impose on farmer earnings[^12][^34].  
The value distribution structure across the Kenyan coffee export sector is detailed below:

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Cherry Share (FCS / Estate)** | 70% – 78% (Clean FOB equivalent) |
| **FCS Factory Processing & Administration** | 5% – 10% (capped at 10% by statute) |
| **Milling, Warehousing, & Broker Fees** | 2% – 5% (broker fee ceiling ≈2%) |
| **Exporter Margin (FOB Mombasa)** | 8% – 12% |

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

The specialty sector in Kenya leverages both the NCE auction and the legally authorized "Second Window" direct sales pathway[^1]. High-scoring lots (85 to 90+ score AA and AB grades) command substantial price premiums at the NCE auction or through direct trade contracts negotiated between international green buyers and licensed grower-marketers or cooperatives[^1].  
In direct trade agreements, the sale is operationalized through a CMA-licensed grower-marketer or cooperative broker who holds the legal authorization to sign export contracts registered with the Coffee Directorate[^2]. The buyer pays via the DSS mechanism, which handles export documentation, coffee warrant transfers, and logistics through the port of Mombasa[^2].  
Traceability in Kenya is high relative to other smallholder origins[^1]. Coffee can be traced directly to individual FCS societies, primary washing factories (e.g., Ndaroini, Thiriku), and specific drying lots[^11]. While factory-level traceability is robust, mapping thousands of individual smallholders who deliver cherry to a single factory presents an ongoing administrative requirement for full EUDR plot-polygon compliance[^1].

### **Rwanda**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Rwanda’s coffee production relies on smallholders. An estimated 400,000 smallholder households cultivate coffee across micro-plots averaging roughly 0.5 to 1 hectare (buyer accounts put the average near 1 hectare), typically containing between 200 and 300 coffee trees per farm[^4][^28]. Estate-scale coffee plantations are nearly non-existent, accounting for less than 2% of total national land planted in coffee.  
Land ownership is structured through state-administered title registration. Following national land tenure reforms, smallholders hold formal long-term land-use lease rights (*emphyteutic lease titles*) registered with the Rwanda Land Management and Use Authority (RLMUA). Land holdings are highly fragmented due to high population density and sub-division through family inheritance.  
Labor dynamics are dominated by unpaid household family labor. Tasks such as weeding, pruning, and cherry harvesting are performed by family members. During the peak harvest season (March to June), smallholders engage local day-laborers (*pise*) to pick cherries selectively. Labor costs are paid on a per-kilogram cherry picking rate. Given the micro-scale of Rwandan plots, explicit labor expenditures are low in absolute terms per household, but represent a major labor investment relative to total household land allocation.

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary post-harvest processing in Rwanda is centralized around approximately 300 Coffee Washing Stations (CWS) distributed across key growing zones (such as Nyamasheke, Gakenke, Huye, and Rutsiro)[^3]. Smallholders pick ripe cherries and transport them on the day of harvest to local CWS[^3]. The CWS process cherries using fully washed systems (pulping, overnight fermentation, channel grading, and soaking), as well as expanding natural and anaerobic processing streams[^3][^14]. Parchment coffee is dried on raised bamboo beds under shade and full sun[^3].  
CWS ownership is split among primary farmer cooperatives, private Rwandan entrepreneurs, and multi-station corporate export entities (such as Muraho Trading Company, Baho Coffee, and Rwanda Farmers Coffee Company)[^3]. Dry milling infrastructure is centralized in and around Kigali, where dry mills hull parchment, perform mechanical size grading, density sorting, and optical defect removal, and prepare green coffee for export transport via the northern corridor (Mombasa, Kenya) or central corridor (Dar es Salaam, Tanzania).

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

The Rwandan coffee trade operates under the regulatory oversight of the National Agricultural Export Development Board (NAEB)[^3]. The market structure experienced a structural shift in **June 2023**, when the Ministry of Agriculture and Animal Resources (MINAGRI) **repealed the Coffee Zoning Policy**[^3].  
Originally introduced in 2016, the Coffee Zoning Policy legally required smallholders to sell their cherries exclusively to designated CWS operating within their administrative district[^3]. While zoning guaranteed cherry supply to CWS and encouraged station owners to supply pre-harvest credit and inputs, it created local purchasing monopolies, suppressed farmgate cherry prices, and limited competition[^4][^22][^23].  
The 2023 repeal restored a free-market model for raw cherry purchasing[^3]. Smallholders and independent cherry aggregators (*commissionaires*) are now legally free to transport and sell cherries to any CWS offering the highest price, regardless of geographical boundary[^3]. Delivered cherries are processed at the chosen CWS, milled in Kigali dry mills, and cleared through NAEB for export. While NAEB sets a seasonal minimum cherry floor price (e.g., 480 RWF/kg in 2024), aggressive competition among CWS driven by repeal pushed market cherry prices past 1,000 RWF/kg[^3]. The floor has continued to climb since: NAEB raised it to 600 RWF/kg for 2025 (+25%), with a further 25% increase announced for the 2026 season[^36]. This price escalation significantly increased immediate cash earnings for smallholders[^3]. However, it also introduced cashflow volatility for smaller CWS and allowed informal cherry aggregators to enter the supply chain, occasionally commingling cherries from different hillsides[^3].  
The distribution of export value across nodes in the post-zoning Rwandan coffee sector is estimated as follows:

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Cherry Seller** | 65% – 75% |
| **CWS Operational Processing Costs** | 12% – 18% |
| **Dry Milling & Transit Freight (Kigali to Port)** | 8% – 10% |
| **Exporter Margin** | 5% – 8% |

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

Rwanda’s specialty coffee sector (80+ scoring Bourbon micro-lots, specialized natural and anaerobic fermentations) relies on single-CWS traceability and strict cherry selection protocols[^3]. The disintermediation following the repeal of the zoning policy has created operational challenges for specialty exporters[^3].  
Direct trade agreements are conducted by licensed export entities—such as specialty CWS groups or cooperative unions—holding export licenses issued by NAEB[^3]. International specialty roasters contract directly with export management to secure single-station lots (e.g., Karenge, Rugali, Bukure)[^4].  
Traceability to the specific CWS remains established[^4]. However, plot-level farm mapping required for EUDR compliance has been complicated by the repeal of zoning[^3]. Because farmers can now sell cherries across district lines to nomadic aggregators offering cash premiums, maintaining strict chain-of-custody documentation from individual farm plots to the receiving CWS requires robust digital tracking systems at reception points[^3]. Specialty exporters are deploying digital buying applications to register delivering farmers at the CWS scale in order to maintain EUDR compliance[^20].

### **Indonesia**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Indonesia’s coffee sector is predominantly smallholder-driven, with over 90% of total national output produced by smallholders operating plots averaging 1.0 hectare[^7][^8]. Commercial state-owned plantations (PTPN \- PT Perkebunan Nusantara) and large private estates account for less than 10% of national production, situated mainly in Java (growing Arabica and Robusta)[^7].  
Land tenure arrangements include formal private land titles (*Sertifikat Hak Milik* \- SHM) registered under the National Land Agency (BPN), customary land rights (*Udayat*) in Sumatran highland regions (such as Aceh Gayo, North Sumatra Lintong, and South Sumatra), and forestry margin permits[^7]. In forest border regions surrounding national parks (e.g., Bukit Barisan Selatan), smallholders cultivate coffee under state forestry co-management schemes (*Hutan Kemasyarakatan* \- HKm) or informal land encroachments[^7].  
Labor dynamics rely heavily on household family labor for daily farm operations[^7]. During the harvest season, producers engage hired local pickers (*buruh petik*) paid on a daily wage or per-*kaleng* (standard measurement tin) basis. In key Arabica regions like Aceh and North Sumatra, mutual aid labor groups (*Arisan Labor*) operate during peak picking periods. Labor costs represent 45–55% of farmgate operating expenditures, driven upward by regional rural labor constraints and competing opportunities in palm oil and mining sectors.

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary post-harvest processing in Indonesia is geographically distinct, dominated by the unique ***Giling Basah*** (wet hulling) technique utilized across Sumatra and Sulawesi[^7]. Under *Giling Basah*, smallholders pulp fresh cherries on-farm using small hand-cranked or gasoline-powered pulpers, ferment the pulped beans briefly in buckets or bags, wash off mucilage, and sun-dry the wet parchment for 1 to 2 days until moisture drops to 30–40% (*pergamino basah*)[^7].  
The wet parchment is sold immediately to local collectors (*Tengkulak* or *Toke*), who operate mechanical wet hullers that strip away the parchment layer while the coffee seed is still soft and moist[^7]. The hulled green-bluish beans (*labu*) are then spread on outdoor drying patios or plastic tarps by collectors and regional traders to undergo secondary drying down to the standard 12% export moisture level[^7]. In Java and Bali, conventional fully washed and natural processing methods are more common, utilizing standardized CWS or cooperative processing units[^7].  
Dry milling and export preparation are concentrated in major port logistics hubs: Medan (Belawan Port) for Sumatran coffees, Surabaya (Tanjung Perak Port) for Java and Eastern Indonesian coffees, and Jakarta (Tanjung Priok Port). Central exporters operate large industrial dry mills equipped with gravity tables, size graders, and optical sorters to process the green coffee into exportable grades (e.g., Sumatra Grade 1 Triple Picked).

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

Indonesia’s internal market structure is defined by a multi-tiered collector network dominated by informal local buyers known as ***Tengkulak*** or ***Toke***[^15]. *Tengkulak* hold significant leverage over smallholders by operating as primary coffee buyers, input suppliers, and informal micro-financiers[^15]. During the non-harvest season (*paceklik*), *Tengkulak* extend credit loans and household groceries to smallholders; in return, farmers pledge their upcoming coffee harvest at discounted purchasing prices (*ijon* system)[^15].  
The chain of custody moves sequentially from the smallholder to the village *Tengkulak* (who executes the wet hulling), then to sub-district collectors, regional processing traders (who conduct secondary drying), and ultimately to large export corporation dry mills located in Medan or Surabaya before containerized export[^15][^24]. There is no central state pricing authority or mandatory coffee auction mechanism. Price discovery is governed by international NY C-Contract (Arabica) and London Robusta market fluctuations, adjusted for regional collector margin deductions.  
The share of export value captured across nodes in the Indonesian wet-hulled supply chain is structured as follows:

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Seller (Wet Parchment Value Equivalent)** | 50% – 60% |
| **Village Tengkulak / Collector (Wet Hulling & Transit)** | 15% – 20% |
| **Regional Processing Trader (Secondary Drying & Grading)** | 10% – 12% |
| **Exporter & Port Freight Logistics Hub (Medan/Surabaya)** | 8% – 10% |

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

The Indonesian specialty coffee sector (single-origin Gayo, Lintong, Toraja, and Java Preanger coffees scoring 80+ points) works to disintermediate the *Tengkulak* supply chain[^7]. Specialty coffee models promote fully washed, dry-hulled, or controlled experimental fermentations (anaerobic naturals) managed by smallholder farmer cooperatives (KSU \- *Koperasi Serba Usaha*) or, increasingly, by private specialty mills and estates (e.g., Asman Gayo Mill in Aceh, Frinsa in West Java, Tuang in Flores, Karana in Bali) that have led Indonesia's specialty growth more than the cooperative channel[^7][^29].  
Direct trade agreements operate under Indonesian commercial regulations via licensed green coffee exporters holding an Export Identification Number (APE) and an Exporter Registered Certificate (*Eksportir Terdaftar Kopi* \- ETK) issued by the Ministry of Trade. Specialty cooperatives or direct exporters establish buying stations in producing villages, paying immediate cash premiums for fully ripe red cherries, bypassing wet hulling to produce high-grade dry-hulled or natural coffees[^7].  
Traceability presents challenges in Indonesia[^15]. The multi-tiered blending executed by *Tengkulak*, coupled with informal farming within national park borders (e.g., Bukit Barisan Selatan), creates risk regarding EUDR compliance[^7]. Achieving plot-level polygon mapping requires disintermediating the informal *Tengkulak* network and integrating smallholders into certified cooperative supply chains capable of registering farm boundaries[^15].

## **Strategic Conclusions and Policy Implications**

This structural analysis reveals that global coffee supply chains are experiencing institutional reconfigurations driven by legal reforms, quality differentiation, and global regulatory mandates. Across the six analyzed origins, distinct policy trajectories and structural challenges emerge:  
The deregulation of historical trading monopolies—evidenced by Ethiopia’s Directive No. 433/2019 and Rwanda’s 2023 repeal of its Coffee Zoning Policy—demonstrates a global shift toward direct market integration[^3]. While these measures increase immediate farmgate cherry prices by fostering competition among buyers, they can also disrupt primary processing infrastructure, incentivize opportunistic short-term cherry trade, and complicate plot-level chain-of-custody tracking[^3].  
Kenya’s Direct Settlement System (DSS) under the Capital Markets Authority represents an institutional framework for eliminating payment delays and restricting excessive administrative fees within smallholder cooperative structures[^2]. Automating funds disbursement directly from central clearing exchanges to producer accounts reduces predatory intermediary leverage and speeds settlement, though farmgate liquidity still depends on cooperative pooling practices and the timing of first payments[^30].  
In origins lacking institutional price floors or direct cooperative outreach—such as Indonesia’s *Tengkulak* network and Peru’s unorganized *Acopiador* stream—smallholders remain vulnerable to informal credit locks and misgraded purchasing deductions[^5]. Strengthening producer cooperatives and expanding local micro-finance access are essential preconditions for improving farmgate margin retention.  
Finally, the implementation of the European Union Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) presents an operational challenge across smallholder-dominated origins. Nations with centralized coffee databases and established cooperative mapping (such as Colombia’s SICA) are relatively well-positioned to comply[^1]. Conversely, origins characterized by micro-plot garden systems, informal forestry border cultivation, and multi-tiered collector blending (e.g., Indonesia, Ethiopia, post-zoning Rwanda) face potential supply chain disruptions unless significant resources are directed toward plot-level polygon mapping and digital traceability systems[^3]. Securing the long-term economic sustainability of the specialty coffee value chain requires balancing price transparency with institutional support for primary processing infrastructure, ensuring that quality-tier premiums flow transparently through to individual producers at the farmgate level[^2].
