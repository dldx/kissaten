# **Comparative Structural Analysis of Global Coffee Supply Chains: Economic Architecture, Institutional Dynamics, and Value Distribution Across Ten Major Origins**

## **Executive Summary**

The global coffee value chain represents a complex economic landscape characterised by sharp structural contrasts between high-volume commodity streams and high-value specialty sectors. This report presents an economic and institutional analysis of coffee production and export architectures across ten pivotal origins: Colombia, Peru, Guatemala, Brazil, Ethiopia, Kenya, Rwanda, Indonesia, India, and Vietnam. Guatemala, Brazil, India, and Vietnam each contribute a distinct structural model: a smallholder economy shaped by seasonal labour migration, exporter dry-mill concentration, and standard-risk EUDR benchmarking (Guatemala); estate-scale mechanised integration governed by futures-market logic (Brazil); a post-liberalisation registration-gatekeeper system anchored by *curing* works (India); and a robusta smallholder chain consolidated at the exporter node (Vietnam). These origins embody diverse models of land tenure, primary post-harvest processing, institutional governance, and market power distribution.  
The structural matrix of coffee origin economics ranges from highly centralised state-regulated models to fragmented, market-driven intermediary networks. In origins like Kenya and Rwanda, post-harvest infrastructure has historically relied on centralised Coffee Washing Stations (CWS) or cooperative factories, governed by state regulatory bodies such as Kenya's Capital Markets Authority (CMA) and Nairobi Coffee Exchange (NCE), and Rwanda’s National Agricultural Export Development Board (NAEB)[^1]. Conversely, Colombia, Peru, and Indonesia are predominantly characterised by decentralised, on-farm primary processing (*beneficios húmedos* or micro-milling), leaving dry milling (*trilladoras*) and secondary consolidation as the primary loci of structural power[^5].  
Recent legislative reforms have altered historical trading pathways. Ethiopia's 2017–2020 reform sequence — new articles on the Coffee Marketing and Quality Control Proclamation No. 1051/2017, Council of Ministers Regulation No. 433/2019 (re-establishing the Ethiopian Coffee and Tea Authority), and ECTA's April 2020 coffee sale procedure — dismantled the mandatory requirement for export coffee to pass through the Ethiopia Commodity Exchange (ECX), enabling direct export pathways through vertical integration (direct delivery in force from January 2021)[^9]. Kenya’s implementation of the Direct Settlement System (DSS) under the Coffee Act reforms has sought to bypass predatory marketing agents by automating direct, rapid fund disbursements to farmer accounts[^2]. Meanwhile, Rwanda’s 2023 repeal of its Coffee Zoning Policy unleashed free-market competition for raw cherries, driving up farmgate prices while simultaneously disrupting established CWS supply partnerships and plot-level traceability structures[^3].  
The specialty coffee sector-defined by cup scores of 80+ points, single-estate or single-washing-station provenance, and specialised processing-acts as a disruptive force against conventional commodity channels. Specialty value chains reconfigure traditional supply networks by establishing direct trade protocols, offering quality-tier price premiums that bypass conventional middlemen, and demanding granular plot-level polygon mapping to comply with international regulations such as the European Union Deforestation Regulation (EUDR)[^3]. However, the equitable distribution of Free on Board (FOB) price export margins remains constrained by structural bottlenecks, including regional dry mill monopolies, localised credit-lock mechanisms by informal buyers (*acopiadores*, *tengkulak*), and the administrative overhead of cooperative management[^5]. Practitioner accounts increasingly caution against a caricature of intermediaries, however: middlemen and importers perform genuine aggregation, quality-control, and trade-financing functions, and many "direct trade" claims overstate actual disintermediation[^25].

Several origins extend the comparative picture with systematic exceptions and confirmations. Brazil inverts the report's intermediary thesis: the farmer is the full producer — running estate-scale on-farm wet and dry milling (*rebenefício*) — farmgate capture is the highest estimated here (78–85% of FOB), and the payment-timing lever points upstream, so that in the 2024–25 price spike it was traders (Atlantica, CafeBras) that suffered margin-call distress while growers banked record earnings[^70][^107][^106][^103]. Vietnam shows farmer stockholding power inverting the same thesis during price spikes — farmers out-waited capital-constrained exporters in 2024–25 — and sustains a closed-loop domestic specialty market in which the best coffee may never be exported[^132][^133]. India demonstrates liberalisation-by-attrition: the 1996 end of Coffee Board pooling left durable gatekeeping in registration and *curing* works, with the top 20 exporters controlling an estimated 86% of exports[^118][^122]. Guatemala ties farmgate liquidity to an exit dynamic — the 2019 price collapse and the 2012–13 *roya* epidemic fed out-migration that now structurally constrains harvest labour — while ANACAFÉ's polygon mapping and Enveritas verification place it institutionally ahead of most smallholder origins on EUDR despite its standard-risk classification[^66][^86][^87][^85].

Beyond market structure, the micro-dynamics between farm-level actors — who extends credit, who controls processing, who sets prices, and who absorbs risk — vary systematically by origin. These are treated explicitly in the Actor Terminology section below and in a dedicated dynamics subsection for each origin.

## **Actor Terminology: Farmers, Producers, and Intermediaries**

Because the roles at origin overlap and the vocabulary varies, the actor terms used throughout this report deserve explicit definition. In prevailing industry usage — articulated by exporters such as Alejandro Cadena (Caravela Coffee) and Kenean Dukamo (Daye Bensa Coffee)[^38] — a **farmer** is the person who owns or works the land: planting, pruning, fertilising, and picking. A **producer** is anyone responsible for the coffee through post-harvest processing and commercialisation up to sale to a trader, cooperative, or exporter; producers need not own a single tree, and many top up their own harvest with purchases from neighbouring farms. An **intermediary** (*acopiador*, *tengkulak*, *commissionaire*) grows nothing: their leverage derives from cash liquidity, transport, credit, and information rather than cultivation.

Where the farmer/producer boundary falls is largely a function of who controls post-harvest processing. In Colombia and Peru, smallholders typically run on-farm wet mills and dry to export moisture, making the farmer a full producer. In Ethiopia, Kenya, and Rwanda, farmers overwhelmingly sell raw cherry to centralised washing stations or factories that own fermentation, washing, and drying — so the washing station, the FCS factory, or the cooperative union, not the farmer, is the de facto processing producer. Indonesia sits in between: the farmer pulps and briefly dries, but the collector performs the defining *giling basah* wet-hulling step. The distinction has financial consequences: a producer delivering dry parchment typically earns more than a farmer delivering cherry, since parchment has already absorbed the processing value. The remaining origins complicate the boundary further: Brazil makes the farmer a full producer at estate scale — the only studied origin where the payment-timing lever points upstream, towards traders and away from growers. Guatemala mirrors Colombia's parchment-producing ideal on paper, but cash timing converts most smallholders into cherry sellers who hand processing value to the buyer. India concentrates the producer role in an estate-and-curer axis: small growers sell sun-dried cherry to village traders, while estates run their own pulperies and the *curing* works that finish, grade, and finance export lots. Vietnam leaves drying with the farmer — more than Indonesia's *tengkulak* chain allows — but the *đại lý* collector and the exporter mill own hulling, polishing, grading, and lot identity.

Two cross-cutting patterns emerge. First, **payment timing is the universal lever**: whichever actor pays fastest — the *acopiador* with spot cash, the *tengkulak* with seasonal credit, the trader with instant cherry payment — captures margin from liquidity-constrained farmers, even when a slower payer (cooperative, union, DSS settlement) would ultimately pay more. Second, **processing ownership determines traceability**: where farmers process (Colombia, Peru), plot-level identity can survive to export; where cherry is pooled at a factory or station (Kenya, Rwanda, Ethiopia) or blended by collectors (Indonesia), traceability is station-level at best unless digital systems are layered on. Both patterns directly condition EUDR compliance capacity in each origin.

> **Farmgate units:** every origin weighs and pays for coffee differently, and the units themselves carry power. Colombia prices parchment by the *carga* (125 kg of dry parchment), with cherry bought by the *arroba* (12.5 kg)[^144]; Guatemala's buyers and pickers work in *quintales* of 100 kg of cherry[^71]; Kenya pays pickers per *debe*, a standard bucket[^1]; Indonesia's *kaleng* tin[^7] and Brazil's *caixa* — a 60-litre box, the unit in which CEPEA/ESALQ benchmark internal prices[^103] — are volume measures whose actual weight shifts with ripeness and moisture. Vietnam quotes a price per kilogram of dried bean[^132][^133]. Behind the zoo of units sits the report's recurring pattern: whoever owns the measure — the bucket, the tin, the scale, the moisture meter — controls the conversion, and every farmgate discount is applied in these units.
>
> **From cherry to green: the conversion problem:** farmgate prices quoted in cherry or parchment look wildly out of line with green-coffee export values because each processing step sheds weight. As a rule of thumb, 4–6 kg of fresh cherry produce 1 kg of green coffee, and dry parchment converts at about 1.25 kg to 1 kg (the ICO's 0.8 green-equivalent convention)[^145]. Each origin then runs its own arithmetic: Colombia's FNC buys a *carga* of 125 kg of parchment at yield factor 94 — roughly 94 kg of *excelso* green, with low-grade *pasilla* paid separately[^144]; India's robusta cherry converts at roughly a 2:1 outturn[^118]; Brazil's natural-process cherry yields around 18–24% green[^145]. This is why Guatemala's reference prices — cherry at $32.72–$38.93 against green at $247.53–$256.53 per 60-kg-bag equivalent — describe the same coffee at different rungs of one ladder, not different markets[^65]. Where the buyer controls the conversion, the arithmetic is where farmgate power leaks away: outturn factors, water weight and moisture deductions are invisible to a farmer selling one bag at a time.

## **Origin-by-Origin Deep Dives**

> **Data note:** The "Share of FOB Export Price" tables in this report are directional estimates synthesised from practitioner accounts (green-buyer, exporter, and producer interviews) and trade literature; they are not audited national statistics and should be read as indicative ranges only. All absolute prices quoted in this report are stamped with the year to which they refer. Coffee prices are highly volatile — the ICE Arabica "C" contract roughly doubled between early 2024 and 2025 — so figures from different seasons or years should never be compared directly, and the percentage-of-FOB shares are structural estimates rather than year-specific observations.

### **Colombia**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Colombia's coffee landscape is dominated by smallholder coffee farming. Approximately 95% of the nation’s more than 560,000 coffee-growing families[^37] cultivate plots smaller than 5 hectares, with an overall national average plot size ranging between 1.5 and 2.0 hectares. Medium-sized estates (5 to 20 hectares) account for roughly 4% of production, while large commercial plantations exceeding 20 hectares represent less than 1% of total coffee land area.  
Land ownership is structured around private individual land titles (*escrituras públicas*), backed by national property registries. Ancestral and customary tenure arrangements exist primarily within indigenous reserves (*resguardos indígenas*) located in regions such as Cauca, Nariño, and Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, where land is collectively managed by local councils (*cabildos*).  
Labour dynamics present a fundamental operational challenge to Colombian coffee production. Smallholder operations rely predominantly on unpaid family labour. Medium and large estates rely on seasonal migratory labour (*recolectores*), who move across coffee belts following peak harvesting periods (*cosecha principal* and *mitaca*). Labour availability has contracted due to youth rural-to-urban migration and rising competition from non-agricultural sectors. Agricultural labour costs are tied to the national minimum wage and piece-rate payments per *arroba* (12.5 kg) of harvested cherry. This inflates total production costs, making labour represent up to 60–65% of total farmgate operational expenditures.

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary post-harvest processing in Colombia occurs almost entirely on-farm through decentralised wet-milling infrastructure known as *beneficios húmedos*. Smallholders utilise mechanical micro-pulpers (*despulpadoras*) and small concrete tanks for mucilage fermentation, followed by sun-drying on raised wooden roofs (*paseras* or *elvas*) or parabolic solar dryers. Centralised Coffee Washing Stations (CWS) are rare in Colombia due to rugged topography and established farm-level processing infrastructure. Producers dry their coffee until it reaches a stable moisture content of 10–12%, producing dry parchment coffee (*café pergamino seco*).  
Dry milling infrastructure (*trilladoras*) is heavily centralised and controlled by two major institutional groups: the *Federación Nacional de Cafeteros* (FNC) through its warehousing arm (*Almacafé*), and large private international and domestic export corporations (such as SKN Caribecafe, Carcafé/Volcafe, and Neumann Kaffee Gruppe). Parchment coffee is transported from farmgate to municipal purchasing points, where it undergoes sample grading (*factor de rendimiento*) to determine the yield of exportable green coffee (*café excelso*). Purchases are conventionally priced per *carga* of 125 kg of dry parchment at the FNC's yield factor 94 ("FR 94"), i.e., a carga of parchment is expected to yield roughly 94 kg of excelso green. Power dynamics at the dry mill node favour large exporters who control industrial sizing screens, gravity tables, optical sorters, and container loading logistics at main port terminals (Buenaventura, Cartagena, Santa Marta).

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

The chain of custody in Colombia moves from the farmgate seller directly to two main channels: local private buyers (*acopiadores* or *compradores de café*) or municipal cooperative buying stations (*cooperativas de caficultores*). Local *acopiadores* leverage cash liquidity to purchase parchment directly from farmers, capturing margins by blending lots and exploiting smallholders' immediate cash flow constraints. The parchment is subsequently sold to regional dry mills (*trilladoras*) for final grading and export clearance at the port.  
The institutional framework is governed by the FNC. The FNC operates the *Precio de Garantía* (guaranteed base price), a daily internal base purchase price calculated using a formula based on the New York ICE C-Contract settlement price, the USD/COP exchange rate, and the prevailing Colombian quality differential premium. The FNC's roughly 33 regional cooperatives guarantee to purchase parchment coffee from any registered grower at this transparent price benchmark, effectively setting a market floor that private buyers must match or exceed. In practice, that floor has not always protected growers: in 2018 the FNC price sat below typical production costs, and in 2024 a producer selling through the FNC stood to profit roughly $0.35/lb versus about $0.53/lb through a private exporter; the FNC is itself funded by a 6% levy on coffee exports (the *contribución cafetera*)[^26].  
The breakdown of export value distribution across the Colombian coffee node is estimated as follows:

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Seller (Parchment)** | 75% – 82% |
| **Local Intermediary / Acopiador** | 3% – 5% |
| **Cooperative / Dry Mill Processing** | 6% – 9% |
| **Exporter & Port Logistics** | 4% – 6% |

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

The Colombian specialty coffee sector (80+ cupping scores, single-farm micro-lots, exotic varietals such as Gesha, Sidra, and Pink Bourbon) bypasses standard cooperative aggregation models. Specialty streams eliminate lot-blending at municipal buying stations by establishing direct relationships between roasters, specialised green coffee exporters (e.g., Caravela Coffee, Banexport), and individual producers.  
Direct trade relationships are operationalised legally within FNC export regulations. While the FNC historically held a virtual export monopoly, private firms and individual producers can register as exporters provided they comply with the national export register, obtain sanitary certifications (INVIMA), and pay the mandatory national coffee contribution tax (*contribución cafetera*) per pound exported. In direct-trade contracts, either the specialised exporter or the producer association holds the active export license. The registration burden is nonetheless steep enough that direct producer-level exports remain the exception; even small importers describe the process as "almost impossible" without falling back on the FNC's export services[^33].  
Traceability in Colombia is facilitated by the FNC's *Sistema de Información Cafetera* (SICA) database, which contains polygon mapping and structural data covering the great majority of the country's coffee farms. This system positions Colombia well to comply with European Union Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) mandates, allowing specialty premiums to flow directly to individual growers based on documented lot-level quality and plot-level geographic verification.

#### **5\. Actor Dynamics & Power Relationships**

Colombia's farmgate product is almost always dry parchment: an institutional effort dating to the FNC's founding has equipped even one-hectare farms to wet-mill, ferment, and sun-dry to *pergamino seco*, making the typical smallholder a full producer rather than a cherry-selling farmer. At the *punto de compra*, parchment is bought for cash per *carga* (125 kg), with the price docked or raised by yield factor and moisture content (FNC maximum 12%; specialty buyers typically demand 9.5–11.5%). A recent counter-trend sees buyers re-promoting cherry sales as a farmer convenience — a shift that quietly moves quality determination and value-add downstream to the buyer[^39].

Competition between cooperatives and *acopiadores* is the principal check on monopsony power at the purchasing point: research on FNC-affiliated purchase points finds that the *garantía de compra* — operationalised through Almacafé's exclusively cash-on-delivery purchase mandate — measurably limits local intermediaries' ability to depress farmgate prices. The floor's sustainability is nonetheless politically fragile, as recent disputes over the prorogation of the FNC contract and the export-tax-funded *contribución cafetera* demonstrate. Information asymmetry compounds cash flow leverage: in a survey of Planadas (Tolima) growers, none reported easy access to any of seven decision-critical information types (buyer count, payment terms, quality expectations, transport costs), and many did not view cooperatives as transparent partners[^41]. Trust is further eroded by *cooperativas de papel* — sham cooperatives operating as usurious lenders[^42].

The sharpest evidence of producer-side powerlessness concerns quality premiums: a study of a Colombian quality-programme exporter finds that under ordinary contracts, exporters capture programme gains and fail to pass quality premia back to farmers; only a roaster-imposed vertical restraint obliging the exporter to pay quality-linked premiums at the mill delivered one-third to two-thirds of programme gains to growers[^40]. Specialty buyers increasingly respond with precisely such restraints, layered on top of SICA polygon requirements.

### **Peru**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Peru’s coffee production is managed by an estimated 220,000 smallholder families, with over 85% of total acreage cultivated on farms smaller than 5 hectares (averaging 2.0 to 3.0 hectares). Medium-sized farms (5 to 15 hectares) make up approximately 12% of the landscape, while commercial plantations exceeding 15 hectares represent a negligible fraction of national output.  
Land ownership models consist of formal private land titles (*títulos de propiedad*) managed by the national land agency (SUNARP), alongside customary land possession arrangements (*certificados de posesión*) issued by local municipal authorities or communal peasant organisations (*comunidades campesinas*). In indigenous Amazonian territories across major growing regions such as San Martín, Junín, Amazonas, and Cajamarca, coffee is cultivated within communal land boundaries.  
Labour reliance leans heavily on unpaid family labour during routine maintenance and early harvesting. During the peak harvest season (*zabra*), smallholders hire seasonal migratory workers (*peones*) from neighbouring Andean highland regions. Labour shortages during peak harvest months have become structural due to youth out-migration and regional wage competition. Labour costs are negotiated on a daily wage (*jornal*) or per-basket (*lata*) basis. Harvest labour accounts for roughly 50–60% of total operational costs, placing financial strain on unorganised smallholders who lack operational credit.

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary post-harvest processing in Peru is decentralised and occurs almost exclusively on-farm via individual micro-mills (*beneficios húmedos*). Smallholders use manual or motorised pulpers to process freshly picked cherries, ferment the pulped coffee in small wooden or concrete tanks, and dry the wet parchment on raised drying beds or plastic-covered solar domes (*módulos de secado solar*). On-farm processing results in variable quality, as smallholders frequently market coffee as wet or semi-dry parchment (*pergamino húmedo*) due to drying capacity limitations in humid rain-forest climates. Centralised CWS are virtually non-existent in Peru due to difficult logistics and geographical dispersion.  
Dry milling infrastructure (*trilladoras*) is concentrated in strategic regional transport hubs such as Jaén (Cajamarca), Pichanaki (Junín), Chiclayo, and the port region of Callao/Lima. These dry mills are owned either by private export conglomerates (e.g., Perhusa, Olam, Louis Dreyfus Company) or by primary producer federations and agricultural cooperatives (such as NORANDINO, APROCAFE, and COOPBAM)[^6]. Parchment coffee is collected in interior towns, transported down the Andean passes to coastal dry mills, and milled, screened, optical-sorted, and bagged for export[^6].

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

The Peruvian chain of custody features a divide between private intermediary channels and cooperative pathways[^16]. In the private channel, independent collectors (*acopiadores* or *compradores local*) buy wet or dry parchment at the farmgate or in rural weekly markets[^5]. These intermediaries utilise immediate cash offers and local transport access to purchase coffee from unorganised farmers, often misgrading moisture levels to extract higher margins[^5]. The aggregated parchment is then sold to larger regional traders (*mayoristas*) who deliver to private dry mills owned by export conglomerates before final export through the Port of Callao[^6]. Alternatively, organised farmers deliver parchment directly to primary agricultural cooperatives, which process the coffee through cooperative-owned dry mills and export federations[^6].  
Peru's institutional landscape lacks a central state price-stabilisation body comparable to Colombia’s FNC. Instead, agricultural cooperatives serve as the structural backbone for market access and quality certification[^16][^18]. Peru has become a leading exporter of Fairtrade and Organic certified Arabica coffee[^16][^17]. Cooperatives capture economies of scale, offer pre-harvest credit, and return certification premiums directly to members[^16]. The cooperative record is uneven, however: long-time residents of growing regions describe many cooperatives as "cooperatives in name only" that operate as private buyers and resellers, and organic certification is widely reported to be gamed (certifying a fraction of members' hectares while marketing far more volume as certified)[^35].  
The estimated value distribution across the Peruvian value chain nodes is structured as follows:

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Seller (Parchment)** | 60% – 70% |
| **Local Collector / Acopiador** | 5% – 8% |
| **Regional Trader / Cooperative Operations** | 8% – 12% |
| **Dry Mill & Export Conglomerate** | 10% – 12% |

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

The Peruvian specialty coffee sector (80+ scoring coffees, high-altitude micro-lots from regions like Villa Rica, Cusco, and Jaén) operates by disintermediating the traditional *acopiador* networks[^5]. Specialty buyers partner directly with primary cooperatives (such as Santuario) or independent specialty exporters (e.g., Red Fox Coffee Merchants) that maintain local cupping laboratories and field staff[^5].  
Direct trade agreements are operationalised under Peruvian commercial law by specialty exporters or certified cooperatives that hold active export credentials (*registro de exportador*) with the Ministry of Agricultural Development and Irrigation (MIDAGRI) and SUNAT (customs)[^6]. In direct trade configurations, the co-op or specialised exporter acts as the legal exporter of record, handling dry milling, phytosanitary clearance, and port logistics, while passing quality premiums directly to smallholders based on cupping scores[^5].  
Traceability to the single-farm level faces structural bottlenecks in Peru. Fragmented land ownership, informal customary land tenure, and remote geography impede plot-level polygon mapping necessary for EUDR compliance. Cooperatives with external technical assistance are better positioned to complete farm mapping, whereas unorganised smallholders selling to *acopiadores* risk exclusion from European supply chains due to lack of verifiable plot traceability.

#### **5\. Actor Dynamics & Power Relationships**

Peru's smallholders are producers in principle but frequently cherry or wet-parchment sellers in practice. Drying capacity is the binding constraint: 85% of the crop is harvested between April and July during the rains, and cash needs push farmers to sell cherry or *pergamino húmedo* within hours of washing. Early sellers absorb both a water-weight discount (parchment weight includes residual water) and a quality discount, while buyers — who own the scales and moisture metres — control grading. In the central jungle, farmgate prices around $1.60/kg were reported in 2017 against production costs then requiring roughly $2.50/kg[^44]. Credit-locks reinforce the position: banks reject untitled land as collateral, so farmers borrow from buyers under fixed-price forward contracts that pre-sell the harvest before the C-market can move[^43].

Peru has no state price floor — the government does not maintain national coffee stocks — so farmers face full C-market exposure and the Fairtrade floor is the only quasi-floor. Even that broke in 2024, when cooperatives defaulted on an estimated 100,000 bags after financing could not cover a C-price spike[^46]. Exporter concentration is moderate (top-10 firms ≈45% of export value in Q1 2026, led by Olam, Romex, and CICA, with Volcafe sixth)[^47]. Cooperative dynamics are double-edged: Peru is widely described as the world's leading exporter of certified organic coffee, yet co-ops have excluded the poorest growers (in Satipo, only one of 230 members at one cooperative was indigenous Ashaninka) and members side-sell to private buyers after production shocks, eroding collective bargaining power[^45]. What binds members is the competition over credit as much as price: cooperative pre-harvest facilities match the seasonal cash cycle against *acopiador* lending. Specialty models such as Red Fox Coffee Merchants decouple prices from the C-market entirely, publish base rates for most volume rather than micro-lots only, and take FOB ownership risk — explicitly aiming to raise the whole local parchment market through competition[^5].

### **Guatemala**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Guatemala's coffee landscape is dominated by smallholder production, but it sits atop one of Latin America's most concentrated land distributions. ANACAFÉ's census update (2023–24) counts roughly 125,000 registered coffee producers[^64], of whom smallholders are about 97 per cent of growers and 44 per cent of output; medium producers generate 31 per cent, and large producers — fewer than 400 operations — produce the remaining quarter[^65][^66]. The national coffee area was re-mapped at 376,000 planted hectares in 2023 (up from the long-cited 305,000), farmed across 261 of Guatemala's 340 municipalities; arabica is roughly 98 per cent of planted area, grown almost entirely under shade, and a large share of the more than 100,000 small farmers cultivate under 2 hectares[^65][^66]. Coffee grows at altitudes that made Guatemala the archetypal "strictly hard bean" origin — 81 per cent of MY 2024/25 exports were classified *SHB* — and ANACAFÉ has branded eight regions (Antigua, Atitlán, Huehuetenango, Cobán, Fraijanes, San Marcos, Acatenango, Nuevo Oriente) under its "A Rainbow of Choices" denomination-of-origin system, the first such classification in the coffee world[^65][^67].

Land tenure carries the imprint of the nineteenth-century liberal coffee boom. The Liberal reforms of 1871–77 — declaring communal lands *baldíos* and legalising debt peonage and the *mandamientos* labour draft (Decree 177) — engineered a mass transfer of indigenous communal land to private coffee estates; by the late twentieth century an estimated 65 per cent of arable land was held by 2 per cent of the population[^67]. Modern tenure is formally private (*escrituras*), but in the western highlands much of the coffee landscape is Maya land: an estimated 40 per cent of the population is indigenous Maya, and in cooperatives such as ACODIHUE in Huehuetenango roughly 80 per cent of member coffee is grown by indigenous, largely Mam-speaking women — many of whom acquired titles through civil-war widowhood or the out-migration of male household heads to the United States[^68][^69]. Inheritance subdivision fragments plots, and the *remesa* economy — remittances worth an estimated 15 per cent of the economy — functions as an implicit subsidy keeping land in coffee when farmgate returns do not[^70].

The labour dimension is Guatemala's most distinctive structural feature, and it runs in two directions at once. Internally, indigenous *jornaleros* — including Q'eqchi' workers from Alta Verapaz, per practitioner accounts — descend each harvest to the large south-coast fincas in a system rooted in the 1870s labour drafts; externally, Guatemalan pickers cross annually into Chiapas's Soconusco region to harvest Mexican coffee[^71][^72]. The pickers' economy is stark: in 2017 fieldwork, a picker targeting one *quintal* (100 kg) of cherry per day earned roughly Q35 (about US$4.50) a day, six and a half days a week, without social security, often housed in *galeras*, with children picking alongside parents[^71]. Since the mid-2010s the labour pool itself has drained northward — practitioner accounts from 2023 describe highland towns where only women remain because the men left for the United States — and USDA attributes Guatemala's fallen-cherry losses to a "permanent lack of labor (migration driven)"[^73][^66].

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary processing splits along the estate/smallholder line. Smallholders pulp, ferment, wash, and patio-dry in on-farm *beneficios húmedos*, then sell either dry parchment (*pergamino*) or — under cash pressure — fresh cherry. The cherry option is cheap: ANACAFÉ reference prices in April 2026 put cherry at $32.72–$38.93 per 60-kg-bag equivalent versus $190.41–$197.33 for parchment and $247.53–$256.53 for green[^65]. USDA reports that most small farmers continue to sell as cherry (paid $21–22 per 100 pounds in MY 2023/24) because they cannot hold coffee to parchment quality — handing both processing value and quality control to the buyer[^66]. Larger farms act as private buying points, paying an estimated 10 per cent above middlemen but only if the farmer delivers cherry to the mill, a transport cost that consumes much of the premium[^66].

Dry milling is concentrated where the export business is: Guatemala City and the Pacific corridor, in mills owned by transnational and local exporters — Volcafe's Peter Schoenfeld S.A. (the country's oldest active exporter), ECOM, and domestic specialty exporters such as La Central de Café, which operates a dry mill outside the capital[^74]. Producer ownership of dry mills is rare — processing specialist Lucia Solis states flatly that "in Guatemala it's not very common" — so even farm-processed parchment must pass through exporter milling to become exportable green[^70]. The cooperative alternative runs through FEDECOCAGUA, the Federación de Cooperativas Agrícolas de Guatemala founded in 1969: a second-tier federation of roughly 148 member organisations and about 20,000 smallholder members — some 70 per cent from indigenous communities across Huehuetenango, Cobán, the Verapaces, San Marcos, and Retalhuleu — providing credit, technical support, warehousing, milling, and export, with 33 affiliated organisations Fairtrade-certified[^75][^76].

ANACAFÉ (Asociación Nacional del Café), established under public law Decree 19-69 and funded by service fees on exports, is the sector's institutional spine: it issues export licenses, runs the ANATEC/GIS geospatial and weather-station network, the Analab quality laboratories, the certified seed programme, a collective input-purchasing programme, and the national coffee mapping on which all area statistics depend[^65][^66]. It also administers the coffee trust fund (Fideicomiso del Café, Decree 31-2001, managed by Banrural — about $65 million under management per USDA's 2025 report, and $108 million in capital with $46 million in accumulated interest per its 2026 update — lending at 2 per cent to smallholders) — the sector's main state credit backstop[^65]. Its extension capacity is nonetheless thin for a 125,000-producer sector — roughly 210 agents serving about 27,500 growers in 2025 — and USDA has criticised the 1969 law as outdated, calling ANACAFÉ's export-license gatekeeping "an unnecessary additional trade barrier" that can delay cooperative exporters for years[^65][^66].

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

Guatemala has no auction, no state price floor, and no government-held stocks. Discovery is C-market arithmetic — ICE Arabica futures plus a differential — and ANACAFÉ's published reference prices act as a transparency benchmark rather than an enforceable floor like Colombia's *Precio de Garantía*[^65]. Selling channels fork at the farmgate: farmers sell cherry or parchment to itinerant and village buyers (*compradores* operating from *bodegas*), to estate mills that buy from neighbours, to cooperative collection points, or to exporter purchasing posts — and whoever pays first, wins. Exporter pre-financing is pervasive in both channels: cash advances against standing coffee at fixed prices lock farmers out of C-market upside, and the 2019 crisis exposed what that costs when the market moves against the farmer[^66].

The chain's economics are documented with unusual candour in USDA reporting. In MY 2023/24, farmgate parchment ran $140–160 per 100 pounds uncertified and up to $250 for combined Organic+Rainforest Alliance, while ANACAFÉ warned that the specialty differential had collapsed from above $18 to around $3 per 100 pounds — "an additional disadvantage" for Guatemalan quality coffee[^66]. The cycle then turned: C prices roughly doubled through 2024 and hit record highs into 2025, lifting export value to $1.284 billion in MY 2024/25 (prices above $300 per 60-kg bag) and Guatemala's highest export value in a decade in first-half 2025 (about $994 million)[^65][^77]. The windfall was unevenly transmitted: exporters reported that container values doubling in a year meant the sector needed an estimated additional $400 million of working capital merely to move the same volume — liquidity, not price, became the binding constraint — while 2025 harvest accounts describe price lists changing weekly as exporters struggle to finance forward commitments[^78][^79].

The breakdown of export value distribution across the Guatemalan node is estimated as follows (directional estimate, consistent with the report's other smallholder-parchment origins; no audited national study is available):

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Seller (Parchment or Cherry)** | 68% – 78% |
| **Comprador / Bodega / Estate Mill Aggregation** | 4% – 8% |
| **Cooperative / Dry Mill Processing & Milling** | 6% – 10% |
| **Exporter, Finance & Port Logistics** | 5% – 9% |

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

The specialty sector builds on Guatemala's dual heritage of regional denominations and auction culture. Guatemala was the second country to adopt Cup of Excellence (in the programme's early-2000s expansion), the 2025 national auction set a record of $143.10/lb — among the highest prices in programme history — and ANACAFÉ runs a parallel "One of a Kind" auction for nano-lots and experimental processes[^80][^65]. Legacy estate names — El Injerto and La Soledad among them — dominate competition podiums to a degree that has disheartened newer entrants, a pattern George Howell discusses as an inherited feature of Guatemalan auction culture rather than a meritocratic outcome[^80]. Experimental processing follows the Latin American playbook — fermentation-focused training camps operate in Antigua and honey processing is spreading — while producer-run camps and roaster origin trips function as direct-revenue channels[^81][^82]. Café Imports' Progress Projects channel part of the specialty margin to origin-side welfare infrastructure: in 2025 its beneficiaries included Funcafé (Fundación de la Caficultura para el Desarrollo Rural), the coffee sector's own food-security and rural-development foundation — an importer acknowledging, in its own words, that Guatemala has the second-highest rate of moderate-to-severe food insecurity in Latin America[^83].

**EUDR compliance.** Guatemala presents the EUDR with a hybrid case. The country was classified "standard risk" (not low-risk) in the Commission's first benchmarking list of 22 May 2025 — meaning coffee exports to the EU (roughly a quarter of Guatemalan exports) face full due diligence at a 3 per cent check rate[^84]. Yet institutional readiness is unusually high for a smallholder origin: ANACAFÉ's ANATEC/GIS system produced a polygon-level national coffee map by 2023–24 (surveyors editing more than 136,000 production sites), and in February 2024 ANACAFÉ signed a memorandum with JDE Peet's and Enveritas — the first such country-level agreement in Latin America — under which Enveritas independently verifies deforestation-free production after the 31 December 2020 cut-off and JDE Peet's finances remediation reforestation where breaches are confirmed[^66][^85]. The asymmetry mirrors the report's other origins: organised cooperatives and estate exporters can ride institutional rails (polygon data, Enveritas verification, co-op-level registration), while unorganised smallholders selling cherry through *compradores* depend entirely on downstream aggregators to attach plot geolocation to their bags — and smallholders, whom ANACAFÉ itself puts at 96–97 per cent of growers, have the least administrative capacity to maintain per-farm records[^85][^65].

#### **5\. Actor Dynamics & Power Relationships**

Guatemala's farmgate seller is a farmer-turned-partial-producer. On-farm *beneficios* make parchment production technically universal, but cash timing converts most smallholders into cherry sellers: cherry is paid the day the bag changes hands, parchment requires weeks of drying capital and exposure to buyer moisture deductions, and the April 2026 reference prices quantify the stakes — a farmer selling cherry receives roughly one-sixth of the bag-equivalent value of green export coffee[^65][^66]. The buyer who pays same-day cash — the *comprador* with his *bodega*, or the estate mill — captures both the processing margin and the grading power, the same liquidity-leverage pattern the report traces in Indonesia's *tengkulak* and Colombia's *acopiadores*. Payment timing proved decisive in the 2019 collapse: when the C price touched $0.86/lb — down roughly 60 per cent from its 2015 level — against production costs many growers could not cover, exporter and middleman advances became the only working capital in the system, and growers in western Guatemala walked away from the crop towards the U.S. migration route[^86]. The 2012–13 *roya* epidemic set the stage: leaf rust infested an estimated 59–70 per cent of national coffee area and Guatemala declared a national agricultural emergency in February 2013, and the subsequent wave of abandonment and renovation debt left producers with less equity when the price collapse arrived[^87]. The 2024–25 record prices reversed the signal but not the structure: with exporters themselves capital-constrained, the fastest payer still dictates terms at the farmgate[^78].

Processing ownership tracks the estate/co-op divide. Well-capitalised family producers — the Caballero family supplying roasters such as Tim Wendelboe, or José Morales in Guatemala, whose large wet mill manages several conventional and organic farms side by side — control wet milling and drying and sell traceability and quality directly to roasters: for the Caballeros, being "in control" of picking, processing, and drying is "a hallmark of Caballero coffee," and Morales's organic deliveries arrive visibly denser per quintal[^88][^89]. Cooperatives aggregate members' parchment but rarely reach the dry-mill/export node except through FEDECOCAGUA, and producer-owned dry milling remains rare enough to be remarkable[^70][^75]. The exporter-dry-mill complex is therefore the de facto processing counterparty for most of the crop — the actor whose screens, grading, and export registration define what "Guatemalan coffee" is at FOB. The farmer/producer boundary falls differently by channel: estate families and specialty exporters are full producers; cherry-selling *campesinos* are farmers whose processing value is captured by whoever buys wet cherry.

The overlay of labour and ethnicity cuts across both dynamics. The picking workforce — indigenous *jornaleros*, child labour documented on both Guatemalan fincas and Chiapas farms, and migration thinning the very labour pool — sits beneath the smallholder "producer" as a further subaltern layer, and the U.S. Department of Labor still listed coffee among Guatemalan goods produced with child labour as of its 2013 findings[^71][^72][^67]. Finally, the border itself is porous in the value chain's favour: practitioner accounts note that "way more Antigua Guatemala coffee is being sold than can possibly be grown in Antigua Guatemala," with Honduran coffee crossing the lightly controlled Guatemala–Honduras border to be re-labelled at a premium — regional denomination, the core of Guatemala's specialty identity, is enforced by exporter self-policing rather than state inspection, and traceability claims rest on the same buyer-side systems (and incentives) as everything else in the chain[^33].

### **Brazil**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Brazil is the structural counterweight to every other origin in this report: the world's largest coffee producer for more than 150 years, currently roughly 30% of global output[^70][^90], an estimated 8 million jobs, and some 330,000 producing properties[^70]. Farm size spans the entire spectrum of global coffee — from plots under 5 hectares in the mountainous *Matas de Minas* to single estates with up to 5,500 hectares of planted coffee producing around 7,000 tonnes a year, more than the entire national output of Panama or Bolivia[^70]. Arabica is concentrated in Minas Gerais (≈1.22 million hectares, about half of national production), São Paulo, and southern Bahia, while robusta — known in Brazil as *conilon* — is concentrated in Espírito Santo (≈433,000 hectares; about 70% of national robusta) and Rondônia (≈95,000 hectares)[^91][^92]. Production for the 2025/26 marketing year was forecast at 65 million 60-kg bags (40.9 million arabica, a record 24.1 million robusta), with Conab forecasting a record ~66.7 million bag crop for 2026 on 2.34 million hectares (private estimates run higher, at 73–76 million)[^92][^93].

Tenure is private fee-simple ownership — *fazendas* hold title deeds and are free to buy, sell, and mortgage land — with environmental obligations layered on top through the federal *Cadastro Ambiental Rural* (CAR) registry, which requires every rural property to declare its perimeter and native-vegetation reserves (a biome-dependent legal-reserve minimum: 20% in southern Minas, 35% in the Cerrado, up to 80% in the Amazon biome)[^70][^116]. Labour is hired, not familial: estates and mid-sized farms contract seasonal picking crews paid per *caixa* of harvested cherry — the unit in which CEPEA/ESALQ benchmark internal prices[^103] — while mechanised operations employ salaried equipment operators — a shift farmer accounts describe as socially progressive (air-conditioned cabs, better pay, skill premiums) even as it erases the picking jobs on which other origins depend[^70]. The history beneath this arrangement is migration: after abolition in 1888, European and then Japanese immigrant labour built the *fazenda* workforce of the early twentieth century, a movement that also seeded Brazil's food-fermentation traditions[^94]. The sector also carries a legacy it does not fully control: the Ministry of Labor rescued 302 workers from coffee plantations in 2023 — the most of any agricultural commodity that year — and 214 in 2024, among them ten debt-bonded workers at Espírito Santo's Mata Verde farm (a Nestlé supplier, May 2023) and 24 more from three southern Minas farms in 2025, an episode that provoked open congressional attacks on the inspection system[^95][^96][^97][^98].

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Brazil processes where most other origins' farmers do — on the farm — but at estate scale and with the wet mill and dry mill both inside the farm gate. Virtually all arabica is prepared as natural or *pulped natural* (***descascado***) coffee: cherry is washed, sorted by density, and dried whole or after skin removal on concrete patios (*terreiros*) and in mechanical dryers, a dry-process tradition that defines the "Brazilian" cup profile the commodity market expects[^91][^70]. Harvesters are vibration-tuned for ripeness, and on-farm wet mills re-sort machine-picked cherry into maturation-based lots by weight and Brix before drying — quality control, not the picking method, is the declared driver of cup quality[^70]. This architecture underpins Brazil's productivity record: between 1960 and 2023, planted area fell from 4.9 to 2.2 million hectares while output rose from 29 to 54.7 million bags — a 55% land reduction with roughly five-fold yield growth driven by government- and university-backed agronomic science (the IAC breeding programme that later identified the low-caffeine AC1 arabica at Fazenda Santa Elisa, Campinas)[^70][^99].

Secondary processing (*rebenefício* — re-milling, size grading, density sorting, defect removal) is the true power node. It is owned by three types of actor: farmer cooperatives — above all Cooxupé, the world's largest coffee cooperative with more than 21,000 members (overwhelmingly smallholders; a 2023 study put small producers at over 97%), generally ranked as Brazil's largest arabica exporter, active across Sul de Minas, *Cerrado Mineiro*, Matas de Minas and Média Mogiana — alongside regional peers Minasul (~6,000 members, ~1.3 million bags per crop, 80% fine-grade) and the Carmo de Minas specialty house COCARIVE[^100][^101][^102]; vertically integrated estates that export directly (Fazenda Mió runs its own wet mill, dry mill, and a UK importing arm opened in 2020; Nova Aliança ships 6,000–8,000 bags with fewer than 30 full-time staff); and multinational traders (ECOM, which rotates trainees through Brazilian exporters such as Capricornio Coffee; Volcafe; NKG; ofi; Sucafina)[^70][^103][^104]. Brazil is also the world's largest instant-coffee exporter (10–20% of coffee exports, 71.5% spray-dried), a demand sink when green prices are unattractive[^91][^92].

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

Brazil has had no state coffee body since the *Instituto Brasileiro do Café* (IBC) was abolished in 1990 after the 1989 collapse of the International Coffee Agreement's quota system — the reason the sector is governed today by markets rather than institutions[^91]. Price discovery runs through the ICE New York "C" contract for arabica and London robusta futures, with internal prices quoted as C ± differential and benchmarked domestically by CEPEA/ESALQ indices per *caixa* of cherry; top-5 export destinations in 2024 were the US (15%), Germany (14%), Italy (7%), Belgium (6%), and Japan (6%)[^103][^92]. The characteristic farmer behaviour is not selling spot but **forward-selling**: bank-financed growers with reserve capital watch the C-market and time their price locks, which is why Brazilian producers explicitly prefer differential pricing that fixed-price specialty buyers elsewhere take for granted[^103].

That hedging culture cut both ways in the 2024–25 price spike. When arabica futures surged above $3/lb on 29 November 2024 — the highest level since 1977, with intraday prints above $3.35/lb that week — traders who had pre-committed forward contracts faced ruinous margin calls, posting an estimated $7 billion in a single month (a Bloomberg estimate); on 27 November 2024 the Brazilian traders Atlantica and CafeBras announced court-led debt restructuring, citing poor crops, heavy rollovers and defaults, and some 900,000 bags of undelivered farmer commitments[^105][^106][^107], and through early 2025 traders who had bet on a price drop scrambled to cover commitments[^108]. Growers, by contrast, earned records: internal arabica averaged $430 per 60-kg bag in April 2025 (+108% year-on-year), January–March 2025 export revenue rose 54% to $3.89 billion, and late-2025 C-peaks above $4.20/lb delivered differential-model earnings practitioners describe as two to three times prior-year levels — reinvested in worker housing, schools, and shade[^92][^103]. The state's role is residual but real: the Funcafé credit line made R$1.17 billion available for 2024/25 (crop management, marketing, working capital) within the broader Plano Safra credit cycle, and most certified commodity volume moves under 4C and Rainforest Alliance — a dominance widely asserted in trade commentary but not independently quantified here[^92]. The estimated value distribution across the Brazilian export node is structured as follows (a directional estimate consistent with this report's other origins):

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Seller (Dried Cherry / Parchment)** | 78% – 85% |
| **Cooperative / Dry Mill Processing (*rebenefício*)** | 4% – 7% |
| **Multinational Trader / Exporter Margin** | 4% – 6% |
| **Inland Transport, Certification & Port Logistics (Santos)** | 3% – 5% |

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

The specialty movement is Brazilian in origin: Cup of Excellence's first auction — "Best of Brazil," 1999, run by George Howell and Susie Spindler — put eleven lots up for sale and Norwegian buyer Solberg & Hansen bought seven of them at roughly $2.50–3.00/lb, at that point without a functioning market for them[^109]. The template it established survives: estate-branded lots (Daterra in the Cerrado is the archetype — a Harvard-educated investor's "gigantic farm" whose field-by-field sampling and cupping George Howell describes admiringly, even while judging that most of its volume is "not that good"), cooperative microlots from Mantiqueira (COCARIVE), and juries that still put plain *Caturra* lots in top-ten positions[^110]. Quality infrastructure is institutionalised: cupping follows BSCA standards — Brazilian research even produced the field's best-known refutation of Brix as a quality proxy (Silva et al.'s Brix/reflectance study, cupped to BSCA protocol)[^111] — and in May 2025 the BSCA signed an MOU making the SCA's Coffee Value Assessment the official protocol for Brazilian specialty coffee[^112]. Practitioners note the ceiling honestly: Brazil produces "fantastic quality in incredible volume," but an 86-point lot demands everything going right, and the baseline product is a "consistent 84 points" espresso-blend profile whose very consistency climate change now threatens — 2024 winter temperatures of 30–31°C pushed maturation from underripe to overripe without ripeness ever arriving[^104][^70][^113]. Brazil was also an exporter of varieties rather than just bags: the Maragogipe mutation from Bahia entered Scott Laboratories' Kenyan germplasm in the 1930s, and named-producer traceability dates to the modern import trade's very start — Cafe Imports' first-ever import, in 1993, was coffee from a single Brazilian producer[^114][^27]. Two frontier movements matter: fine robusta/*conilon* — served in São Paulo specialty cafés, with Brazil credited as the leader in rehabilitating robusta's reputation — and farm-level vertical integration into importing (Mió's London arm) and roaster-funded on-farm research (Project Sombra shade trials; Project Baja quality mapping)[^115][^70].

**EUDR compliance** is Brazil's most asymmetric regulatory test. On paper the country is superbly positioned: the CAR registry already supplies georeferenced farm polygons and native-vegetation status for nearly every property, underpinning traceability platforms such as SeloVerde MG, and thereby settles both the geolocation and legality prongs of the regulation for estate and cooperative supply chains[^116][^117][^70]. The binding constraints are elsewhere: Brazil must prove no deforestation after 31 December 2020 on every declared polygon — a bar the Cerrado, western Bahia, and Rondônia frontiers face more often than old-established Sul de Minas — and under Regulation (EU) 2025/2650 (30 December 2026 for large and medium operators, 30 June 2027 for micro and small) the burden falls hardest on the smallholder tail feeding cooperative buying stations, where commingled lots and a missing or self-declared CAR polygon can silently exclude a municipality's micro-lots from EU-bound contracts[^117][^103]. Because the farm is the producer node in Brazil, compliance costs can be internalised where the farm is an estate — and externalised nowhere in particular where it is a 3-hectare plot.

#### **5\. Actor Dynamics & Power Relationships**

Brazil inverts the power geometry of every other origin in this report. The farmer is the producer — full stop: the *fazenda* pulps, dries, re-mills, and increasingly exports its own coffee, so the actor selling at the farmgate is the same actor who owns post-harvest processing, the precise configuration Kenya's FCS factories, Rwanda's CWS, and Indonesia's *Tengkulak* deny their farmers[^70]. Power therefore runs downward, from farm to worker: the decisive farmgate transaction is not grower-versus-buyer but estate-versus-piece-rate picker, with *caixa* rates, housing, and labour-law compliance as the live negotiation surfaces — and with slavery-like conditions, documented in 302 (2023) and 214 (2024) government rescues, marking the coercive end of that continuum[^96][^95]. The payment-timing lever likewise points upstream: because growers hold differential-priced forward contracts and the liquidity to time sales, the liquidity-vulnerable actor is the intermediary — it was traders Atlantica and CafeBras, not growers, whose forward commitments triggered margin-call distress and court-supervised restructuring when the C-market doubled through 2024–25[^107][^105].

Downstream, the chain is thin but concentrated: Cooxupé alone controls the largest share of arabica exports, and six-plus multinationals compete for the commodity flow; the spread between internal *caixa* prices and FOB is compressed by competition among co-ops, estates, and traders all buying the same dry cherry — which is why the estimated farmgate capture (78–85%) is the highest of any origin in this report and why practitioner accounts treat Brazilian farmers as price-setters in all but name[^100][^103]. The residual asymmetries are informational and climatic rather than transactional: smallholders in Matas de Minas and Espírito Santo depend on co-op pricing sheets for market knowledge, high C-prices mechanically reward strip-picking over selective harvest (exporters' classic "pay more, pay quickly, pay in full" discipline must be re-purchased in every spike), and Brazil's own scale anchors every other origin's price — competing origins' differentials are quoted against a market Brazil dominates, because even when Peru's crop collapsed to leaf rust, Brazil kept pumping coffee and the price stayed down[^35]. For EUDR and traceability purposes, Brazil is the only studied origin where plot-level polygons are a farm's own administrative record rather than a project's engineering task — compliance, like production, scales with farm size.

### **Ethiopia**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Ethiopia's coffee sector is anchored by an estimated 2.2 million smallholder households producing more than 95% of national output, on micro-plots averaging under 0.9 hectares across four systems (forest, semi-forest, garden, and plantation coffee); state-owned or private plantations are under 5% of production. Practitioner accounts put holdings even smaller: Cafe Imports describes farms averaging under 2 hectares, with the garden-coffee norm at 50 to 100 rarely-pruned trees per household, intercropped with corn, enset, and chat[^27][^156]. Gedeo inheritance follows male lines — holdings "typically passed on to male children, most of which are less than a hectare" — making Tigest Wako's 7.6-hectare inherited farm the outlier that single-farm sourcing celebrates[^27]. The estate tier is thin but established: Tega & Tula, one of the country's oldest specialty estates, has been revitalised under Ahadu Woubshet since 2015[^162].

Land is the public property of the state; farmers hold hereditary land-use rights (*holding certificates*) permitting cultivation and inheritance but prohibiting sale or mortgaging. For forest-zone producers the operative instrument is the long-term lease — roughly 100 years per a 2023 practitioner account[^156] — and Kaffa's Tatmara Coffee Plantation traces the paperwork path: lease filed 2008, planting begun 2010, holding grown from 50 to more than 82 hectares[^154]. Scale figures are loosely quantified: Tatmara's founder describes Kaffa as "more than 450,000 hectares of coffees" (a speaker-approximate figure, best read as an order of magnitude), against a JARC catalogue of "about 5,000 species" of which 44 selected varieties are cultivated[^154].

Kaffa's production is organic by default rather than by certification: fertiliser is effectively unavailable because the hard-currency shortage prices it out of reach, so most gardens run on zero inputs, and the leaf-rust and coffee-berry-disease pressure of other origins is largely absent under forest shade[^156][^154]. Trade-offs are wildlife and land competition: baboons destroy an estimated 25–30% of the crop on forest farms (a hedged figure), eucalyptus claims water-hungry plots, chat is more profitable than coffee, and youth are exiting — "young people don't want to grow coffee"[^154][^156].

Labour runs on family networks, supplemented during harvest by reciprocal labour-sharing arrangements (*Dabo*) among neighbouring households. Because nearly every local family also owns garden coffee, larger farms hire afternoon-only shifts after workers finish their own plots, with an on-site bakery as an in-kind wage supplement; Tatmara needs roughly 200 pickers against 50–70 planters[^154]. Cherry-picking rates roughly doubled year-on-year in the early 2020s (about 6 to 12 per kilogram between 2022 and 2023, units unstated by the speaker), pushed by competition from construction wages near Bonga[^154]. Commercial plantations and larger outgrower schemes hire seasonal migrants from neighbouring regions on daily wages or per-kilo cherry-picking rates. Explicit labour costs are often absorbed into household subsistence economies, though rising living costs keep pushing harvest wages upward.

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary processing follows two pathways: washed and natural. Washed coffee (roughly 30–40% of production) is processed at centralised Coffee Washing Stations (CWS) operated by private suppliers or Primary Farmers' Cooperative Unions (e.g., Yirgacheffe, Sidama, Oromia), to which smallholders carry fresh cherries on foot or by pack animal[^10]. Natural coffee (60–70% of output) is sun-dried on raised beds — on-farm for the smallest growers, though most export naturals are processed at stations running washed and natural streams side by side[^10].

Dry milling — hulling, destoning, grading, optical sorting, bagging — is concentrated in regional hubs (Jimma, Agaro, Hawassa, Dilla) and Addis Ababa's industrial zones[^10]; historically, all green coffee had to be transported to central warehouses in Addis Ababa for mandatory government inspection and grading, and the mills remain owned by private exporters, primary cooperative unions, and state-backed agricultural enterprises[^9]. The geography is a structural constraint for southwestern small producers: Tatmara's own cherry-husk dry mill (installed 2023) covers preliminary work, but final export milling must still be done in Addis Ababa, roughly 470 kilometres away, where mill queues run "one month, two months," small producers are deprioritised behind large three-container farmers, and coffee can be "swapped… [or] blended" in the stack — why green buyers insist on staying "on top of the coffee until it leaves the country"[^154]. Regulation compounds the logistics: internal transport bags are 85 kg while export bags are 60 kg, so natural lots need three bags and washed lots two for the same green weight, with GrainPro liners used farm-to-port[^154].

The bottleneck shapes buyer-protocol sourcing. The roughly 30-hectare HMO/Chemo farm in Agaro doubles as a cherry-buying wet mill; under the 2024 protocol each day's picking was kept separate — about 25 lots in GrainPro with moisture checks, cupped, and held to an 84.5-point floor, acceptable lots blended to roughly ten, the first washed samples scoring 87–89.5[^156]. Dollar scarcity forced the purchase of a used Penagos eco-pulper from a cooperative — a diffusion pattern repeating across the west — and dry milling moved from the government Horizon mill to Geisha Village in Bench-Maji, which vacuum-packs before shipment[^156].

At scale the same discipline applies: Ardent's Idido station (2,200 metres) serves roughly 1,200 producers and rests washed coffee six weeks before milling — the 2025 Malebo project was its only washed lot that season[^160]; Testi's Yaye station works 50 smallholders on a single variety (74518), hand-sorted to roughly 95% full-ripe, dried 28 days to 12% moisture, and rested six weeks before GrainPro delivery to Testi's Addis mill[^161]. These protocols sit inside the ECX traceability problem: Cafe Imports estimates that "traceability for 80% of Ethiopia's production was lost once aggregated by the ECX," with the traceable remainder being estates of 35+ hectares (holding direct-export rights) and cooperative unions, sold FIFO without pre-shipment samples[^27].

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

The institutional framework shifted through a 2017–2020 reform sequence — new articles on Proclamation No. 1051/2017, Regulation No. 433/2019 re-establishing the Ethiopian Coffee and Tea Authority (ECTA), and ECTA's April 2020 sale procedure[^9]. Before 2019 the ECX (founded 2008) mandated that all commercial coffee pass through its auction floor[^10], destroying traceability by commingling coffee into regional grades (e.g., Sidamo Grade 2, Yirgacheffe Grade 1) — the standard channel still carries no farm-level geolocation[^27][^10]. Grading ran by quality, "not where they're from. So a coffee from Jimma might end up as a Sidama 2," and traceability was only sideways[^156]. Adjacent econometric work finds traceable lots' export prices rose faster than non-traceable lots' after the ECX — commingling made traceability scarcer and more valuable[^51].

The pre-reform route was equally restrictive for small roasters: until about 2018, the only certain channel ran through cooperative unions, whose lot minimums — "in some cooperatives 50 bags, but most cooperatives 150 bags, so half a container" — were too large for small roasters[^156]. The 2018–2020 opening changed the rules: exporters may own and operate washing stations and bypass the ECX, cooperatives may export without a union or the ECX, smallholders with 2-plus-hectare export licenses may sell directly, and smaller growers may pool; Lulo Coffee (Kidane family, 2019) acts as agent for 30 export-license-holding smallholders across stations in Guji, Sidama, Yirgacheffe, and West Arsi[^27][^156]. The April 2020 procedure formalised the direct pathways[^9]: registered suppliers, station owners, and unions may bypass the ECX floor and sell to international buyers directly, so cherries flow straight to the exporter and onto FOB Djibouti, with exporters able to provide advance working capital to local suppliers[^9][^10]. Vertically integrated grades 1–4 are capped at up to 5 per cent above ECX premiums, but commercial coffee (grades 5–10 and undergrade) has traded at up to 22 per cent increments over the ECX premium — figures from a single trade-account source[^9][^10]. Uptake among the smallest growers was limited: "in reality, smallholders lacked the language fluency, credit line, and infrastructure," so most continued selling cherry the old ways[^27].

The reform era added a price-discovery system: ECTA publishes weekly minimum registration prices for exportable coffee by grade, region, process, and producer type, and every contract must be registered with the government to export and to receive foreign currency[^26]. The formula factors cherry prices and birr strength and lags the C-market; in December 2024 the minimum for washed Guji Grade 1 was roughly $4.50/lb while top lots signed at $5–11/lb FOB[^26]. Minimums compress grades: Grade 2s traded only $0.10–0.20/lb below Grade 1s, so few were produced and their volume was "bulked into Grade 1 offers"[^26]. Price behaviour is distorted by the foreign-exchange motive: because coffee is Ethiopia's main dollar earner, exporters have sold green coffee "maybe with a loss even but just to get U.S. dollars," profiting instead on the imports those dollars buy[^156]. The same logic lets large roasters impose price caps — one large Norwegian roaster buys Sidamo Grade 2 under a cap, and when prices rise "they might actually be sent to grade three or grade four"[^156].

The value distribution across the vertically integrated Ethiopian coffee node is outlined below:

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Cherry Seller** | 55% – 65% |
| **Primary Processor / CWS Supplier** | 12% – 18% |
| **Dry Milling & Freight (Addis to Djibouti)** | 10% – 14% |
| **Exporter Margin (Port Djibouti)** | 8% – 12% |

Two cautions attach: the farmgate figure describes the vertically integrated stream alone (the 14–38% all-channel average is discussed below), and the export statistics cited here are gross values at port, not farmer net receipts — processing, milling, freight, and financing intervene between the FOB price and the cherry price.

Recent export data must be read with the same series discipline. USDA FAS forecasts 2025/26 production at 11.6 million 60-kg bags and exports at 7.8 million, against an earlier 2024/25 estimate of roughly 7.0 million[^146]; for 2024/25, ECTA's fiscal-year account gives 468,967 tonnes and US$2.653 billion[^147], while a later USDA/FAS coffee-year account gives 7.43 million bags and US$2.89 billion[^148]. These are not interchangeable measures — fiscal-year tonnes/receipts versus coffee-year bags, apparently a later revision — and the discrepancy is a measurement/calendar issue until the ECTA monthly ledger is released[^146][^147][^148]. Saudi Arabia and Germany remained the two largest 2024/25 destinations, with China third, then Belgium and the United States[^148]. No source supplies a defensible arabica/other-species split; Ethiopia's export is overwhelmingly arabica, but that qualitative statement should not become a number without an ECTA or USDA table. Nor does the export-value increase establish farmgate pass-through: sources report national exports, not a linked panel of cherry, parchment, and exporter receipts, and the 2025/26 forecast's reform-and-price attribution is unmatched by a verified count of ECTMS-mapped farms or registered exporters[^146].

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

The reform sequence altered the specialty landscape[^9]. Specialty coffees (scoring 80 to 90+ points from Guji, Yirgacheffe, Sidama, and West Arsi) can now bypass commodity blending and maintain plot or washing-station provenance[^10]. Direct trade runs through licensed exporters or Primary Cooperative Unions holding ECTA-issued export permits[^9]; international roasters contract directly, specifying cupping profiles, preparation standards (e.g., Grade 1 zero-defect hand sorting), and station locations[^10].

High prices have a documented downside at the cherry-buying line. Where stations compete for ripe cherry, the exporter is the direct beneficiary of high prices, and competition relaxed sorting and ripeness standards and even elevation separation; average cup scores fell and quaker counts rose between November 2024 and January 2025, with the most extreme effects suppressed only by liquidity constraints before the birr devaluation[^26].

The single-farmer premium mechanics predate the reform era: at the Single Farmer Project's 2012 launch the model paid roughly $3.40/lb FTO plus a $0.45/lb premium handed to the farmer in birr; of more than 40,000 Yirgacheffe union members only three farms could fill separable 30–60-bag lots, and the programme lapsed between 2015 and 2019 before reviving under a vertically integrated exporter[^27]. The current tier is built on washing-station protocols rather than single-farm lots: Malebo (2025) was Idido's only washed lot, fermented 36 hours in purified water and dried ten days to 11.2% moisture, with $0.10/lb donated to the Idido Children's Center — one of two Ardent-run centres (Bensa and Yirgacheffe) serving 70 children[^160]; Odakko (2025) was a 50-producer, single-variety Sidama lot dried 28 days under shade net, with Testi and the local Ministry of Agriculture distributing *Ficus vasta* seeds in the Arbegona district[^161]. Cafe Imports' observation that "by design, microlots, or even small, focused lots, are uncommon" captures the structural reality: the washing station, not the farm, is the unit of quality and identity[^161].

Buyer-financed capital is how the tier pays for processing it does not own. Tim Wendelboe's roastery crowdfunded Tatmara's micro wet mill at its fifteenth-anniversary pay-what-you-want day — about $5,500 raised, topped up by the roastery to roughly $10,000, with Paris importer Terres de Café adding about $4,000, against a total cost near $17,000 after inflation — and the first washed harvest landed in 2023/24, with the roastery's partner floor price set at $5/lb[^154]. Belco's Ethiopia team functions as the on-the-ground enforcement arm of roaster protocols, a role the sector's institutions do not play: JARC is widely described as under-resourced — "more collection than breeding"[^156].

Traceability to the single station or cooperative level is established under vertical integration[^9], but plot-level farm mapping for EUDR compliance presents administrative and logistical hurdles: because millions of smallholders cultivate micro-plots in dense agroforestry garden and forest environments, polygon mapping requires immense technical resources, and unorganised smallholders outside union networks risk supply-chain exclusion if plot-level verification cannot be established before EUDR enforcement deadlines.

#### **5\. Actor Dynamics & Power Relationships**

Ethiopian smallholders are almost pure cherry farmers: cooperatives buy roughly one-third of red cherry but only about 10% of coffee farmers are members, receiving a spot base price plus a dividend roughly three months later; unions retain about 30% of net profits (defended as covering processing, certification, and finance) and return 70% as dividends and development funds[^49]. Private suppliers (*akrabis*) pay instant cash — often above the cooperative spot price — motivated less by coffee margins than by access to hard currency for the import market. Grading opacity compounds the asymmetry: in recent Sidama fieldwork, only 2 of 55 farmers understood the grading system used to price their coffee. The result is strikingly low farmgate capture — an estimated 14–38% of export price over the past fifteen years (a 15-year all-channel average[^48]; the 55–65% share in the value table describes the vertically integrated stream alone) — and post-2017 exporter competition, with the exporting base expanding from fewer than 50 firms, coincided with a *falling* farmer share as gains flowed to exporters chasing foreign exchange[^48].

The single-farmer channel illustrates the fragility of direct relationships: the model lapsed when regulatory risk rose before reviving under a vertically integrated exporter, and at launch only three of more than 40,000 Yirgacheffe producers could fill an exportable micro-lot[^27]. Payment timing remains the farmgate's live lever: in late 2024 producers sought prepayment or financing ahead of shipment payment, and CTA registration ties export and foreign-currency access to contract registration[^26]. Direct relationships are fragile in both directions — Belco first bought from Tatmara in 2017 ("the first time I got foreign currency, very good money"), yet when a rain-damaged 2020 natural lot threatened the partnership, the buyer took 40 bags against the 80 planned at a reduced but above-market price to keep it alive; the buyer's counterpart duty is vigilance: "the most important part now for you is to stay on top of the coffee until it leaves the country"[^154].

Logistics has become a structural risk. The 2024 crop ran about a month late amid road blockages, truck and diesel shortages, and Red Sea (Houthi) attacks that led Maersk to refuse Djibouti calls, forcing rerouting via Singapore and Africa and 2–3 month delays; Kaffa operates under the rule that coffee must be hulled to green beans before transport to Addis[^156]. By 2025 the same 2–3 month shipping window persisted, with vacuum packing used to preserve quality in transit[^157]. At Tatmara in 2025 the accumulated risk changed the product line: after a 2024 harvest whose quality slipped slightly (unseasonal rain, per Belco's Jacques), the partnership stopped producing naturals altogether, and of the 100+ bags planned about 50 were bought, shared with Terres de Café[^157]. The price frame has not moved: with the C-price around $4/lb in December 2025, producers note that green coffee "has been… around the dollar per pound… for many years which means the farmers have the same salary for 50 years," and that high C-prices do not equal farmer profit once fertiliser, labour, food, and fuel costs are subtracted[^157].

For EUDR compliance, Ethiopia's state land-tenure system — which blocks collateral-based credit (under 5% of credit reaches agriculture) — paradoxically eases geolocation: Second-Level Land Certificates feed the ECTA first-mile geolocation app (ECTMS), with Africa's largest cadastre (>32 million parcels) as the backbone[^50]. Operational coverage of ECTMS mapping remains an evidence gap, and the same caution applies to claims that July 2024 foreign-exchange liberalisation uniformly benefited farmers, since the sources found here do not link exporter birr receipts to producer cherry prices.

### **Kenya**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Kenya's coffee sector exhibits a dual ownership structure split between smallholder farmers and estate enterprises[^19]. Smallholders account for approximately 70% of total national production and are organised into Farmers Cooperative Societies (FCS)[^1]; producer-exporter Vava Angwenyi puts the smallholder count at 700,000-plus farmers[^159]. Smallholder plots are small, averaging under 1.0 hectare (frequently containing 200–300 coffee trees per household)[^1], and inheritance subdivision has carried the fragmentation further: practitioner accounts describe land "sectioned out… for their kids" into half-hectare and even quarter-acre plots, with daily deliveries at factory intake running "as little as 200 grams"[^155]. The remaining 30% of production originates from the estate sector — medium-sized estates (5 to 20 hectares) and large plantations (>20 to several hundred hectares) historically managed by private corporations or individual growers (e.g., Gethumbwini, Sasini)[^1].

Land ownership is underpinned by formal private land titles (*title deeds*) issued under the national land registration system. Inheritance practices have progressively fragmented smallholder plots over successive generations[^12]. The producer demographic has aged with the trees: growers are widely described as having "retired into coffee… in their 60s"[^155].

The variety base is a legacy asset under dilution. Commercial coffee has been grown on the virgin volcanic soils of central Kenya only since roughly the 1960s, built on the SL28 and SL34 selections that still define the origin's cup — "the flavor of Kenya is SL coffee," as an ECOM trader puts it — but Ruiru 11 and Batian are progressively replacing them, and the replacements cup out to "yellow fruit, floral… too similar to coffees from other origins"[^155]. Input-dependence is severe: chemical inputs make the "cost of production obscenely high," and a shade study showing 40–60% higher yield under shade has not displaced the input-heavy model[^155].

Labour reliance varies by production sector. FCS smallholders depend primarily on household family labour, supplementing with daily-paid local labour during peak picking[^1]. Estates rely on casual seasonal labour recruited from neighbouring communities for selective harvest, paid by bucket, day, or hour — practitioner accounts note "no real incentive… I've rarely been to a place that pays an additional bonus for people to pick well"[^155]. Labour costs are governed by statutory minimum wage regulations and piece-rate payments per *debe* (standard measurement bucket) of cherry, and labour accounts for over 50% of estate production costs; regional labour shortages during peak harvest months have accelerated production costs across Nyeri, Kirinyaga, Murang'a, and Kiambu[^11]. The urban fringe is converting Nairobi-edge coffee land into warehouses, and youth are moving to office work rather than the farm[^155].

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary post-harvest processing is centralised for smallholders and decentralised for estates[^19]. Smallholders pick ripe cherries and deliver them on the same day to centralised washing factories (*wet mills*) managed by their respective FCS[^2]. These factories utilise multi-disc pulpers, double-fermentation washed systems, channel sorting, and drying on raised wooden beds (panya beds) to produce parchment coffee[^1]. Estates operate their own on-farm wet mills[^1].

The processing protocol itself has drifted from its colonial template, with direct cup consequences. The colonial-era standard was double fermentation (dry then wet) plus soaking, with fermentations running "36, 48 hour plus," up to 72 hours, and soaking holding up to a week; today, cooperative factories "could be maximum a day, often less," many skip the secondary fermentation and do not soak — a drift attributed to water-conservation NGOs, low volumes, and bad engineering (Karagoto built new soaking tanks that proved unusable and were abandoned)[^155]. The flavour register moved with the protocol, from the "big, bold, syrupy… dark fruits" of the classic profile to "yellow, light, green, pinkish" notes[^155]. The pulpers doing this work are often "50 plus years old," and the advice feeding the system from the Coffee Research Institute/Foundation has gone essentially unchanged for 20–30 years[^155].

Milling infrastructure (*dry mills*) has undergone statutory restructuring following the Coffee Act reforms (2023–2026)[^1]. Historically dominated by private multinationals[^19], milling has shifted under a separation-of-roles rule barring a single firm from acting as exporter, broker, and miller simultaneously, towards county-owned dry mills (e.g., Kirinyaga, Kipkelion) and the restructured KPCU, with some private mills closing in a transition practitioners describe as abrupt[^19][^30]. The consolidation is drastic in scale: one 2025 practitioner account illustrates the change with a "200 mills → 10" example, explicitly flagged by the speaker as "not the correct number, so it's just an example," to convey the resulting bottlenecks and the mills' inability to handle the logistics of tiny lots[^157]. Dry mills process parchment into standardised commercial sizes: AA (screen 17/18), AB (screen 15/16), PB (Peaberry), C, TT, T, and E1.

Two downstream frictions compound the bottleneck. The 2024 regulations decoupled the "outturn week" from the harvest itself — the outturn-week number is simply the week the coffee was milled, and "that coffee could have been there for a month" — producing woody flavours in week-18 lots that never spent 18 weeks in the field[^155]. After auction, the exporter chooses a second mill to clean, defect-sort (with electronic sorters), density- and screen-sort, and vacuum-pack before containerisation — another step and another set of charges in the parchment-to-green journey[^158].

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

The Kenyan coffee trading framework is regulated by the Capital Markets Authority (CMA) and the Coffee Directorate, with the weekly public auction at the Nairobi Coffee Exchange (NCE) as the primary price-discovery mechanism[^1]. In the primary stream, FCS smallholders deliver cherries to cooperative factories, which send processed parchment to county or public dry mills[^2]; CMA-licensed independent coffee brokers place the graded coffee onto the NCE floor[^1], and licensed export buyers bid on lots, transferring funds through a commercial clearing bank operating the Direct Settlement System (DSS), which deposits disbursements directly into the accounts of cooperatives and individual farmers[^2].

The auction dominates the flow. Vava Angwenyi's two public estimates bracket its share at "close to 90%" of Kenyan coffee traded at the auction (November 2022) and "95% or more" through the auction window with the remainder in a direct-sales window (December 2022) — the two figures should be read as her two estimates rather than a reconciled statistic[^159][^164]. The auction also benchmarks direct sales: in her 2022 account "the AA this week was sold for $6.5 minimum… that is a benchmark for all direct sales," farmers counter direct offers with auction prints, and direct buyers must improve the offer to win the lot; without the auction, she argues, "people will just be landing in Nyeri and saying, hey, we're buying all the production for $3 a kilo"[^159]. Farmers hold a right of refusal on direct offers — waiting for Tuesday's auction and negotiating from its result — and direct buyers capture a "little bit more higher percentage of that price directly to the cooperative because they don't have to pay the auction fees"[^164][^158].

Under the 2023–2026 reforms, traditional marketing agents were replaced by CMA-licensed independent coffee brokers — including county-based cooperative brokers and specialised brokerage firms, who prepare auction catalogues and represent producers on the NCE floor[^1] — though veteran buyers note the change was partly a re-badging of the same role, with broker fees on the order of 1–2% of sale value[^2][^31]. The pathologies the DSS was built to fix were severe: the dry millers who doubled as marketing agents left farmers with under-five-acre plots "at the mercy of another miller who's also the marketing agent," and "there's marketing agents who've taken off with farmers' money"[^164]. Before the DSS, the law already required farmers to be paid "within seven days of bidding on a coffee"[^164]. Auction access is itself gated — "to be a trader at the Kenyan auction needs a ton of money and a ton of security… It's for the big boys… for the multinationals" — though the NCE offers a transparency counterpoint in its license-holder database and week-on-week reports of what sold for what[^164].

The Direct Settlement System, operated through an appointed financial clearing institution (initially Co-operative Bank, with Stanbic Bank Kenya approved as an additional DSS provider in 2026), mandates that proceeds from auction or direct sales be deposited into a central clearing account and disbursed directly to cooperative societies, mills, brokers, and individual farmer accounts — statutorily within days of settlement[^13][^2]. At the exchange level this automates settlement, but practitioner accounts from 2024 note that farmgate timing still lags: FCS payouts are typically pooled and averaged after the selling season (often months after harvest), and first-payment (cherry advance) systems were not yet functioning, leaving growers short of working capital[^30]. The Coffee Act (Senate Bill 2023, enacted as Act No. 8 of 2026) caps FCS fees to growers at 10% of gross coffee-sales earnings (s. 70(1)(a)) — up from the 5% ceiling discussed during the 2022 reform debate[^12][^34].

The category system itself excludes Kenya's experimental streams. "In Kenya a natural is classified as mbuni… on the coffee catalog you'll find it classified as an MH," and the auction "does not have the category for naturals… it does not have the category for semi-washed," so "they then obliterate whatever a farmer did"; farmers burned once will not experiment "unless you pre-finance the whole process," and unmet demand for Kenyan naturals is visible among Gulf and Saudi buyers[^159]. Buyer geography is similarly narrow: buyers refuse eastern Kenya and concentrate on the central region[^159]. Micro-lot minimum rules persist, with allowances for two-, three-, and five-bag lots at the margin[^164]. When the C-market spiked in 2022, "farmers were laughing all the way to the bank… they're not even listening to direct deals" — the auction, not direct trade, captured the upside[^164].

The value distribution structure across the Kenyan coffee export sector is detailed below:

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Cherry Share (FCS / Estate)** | 70% – 78% (Clean FOB equivalent) |
| **FCS Factory Processing & Administration** | 5% – 10% (capped at 10% by statute) |
| **Milling, Warehousing, & Broker Fees** | 2% – 5% (broker fee ceiling ≈2%) |
| **Exporter Margin (FOB Mombasa)** | 8% – 12% |

These are clean-FOB-equivalent estimates; the auction statistics cited below are gross values, not farmer net receipts — an AA lot's gross price is not what the farmer received after FCS deductions, milling, brokerage, finance, and delayed pooling. The 2025–26 auction record shows the value effect of the price recovery: 41,561,910 kg traded for KSh38.74 billion in calendar 2025, at an average KSh932.19/kg[^149]. Individual sales show dispersion within the trend: Sale 34 (5 August 2025) moved 7,278 bags (455,031 kg) for US$2.851 million, averaging US$313 per 50-kg bag[^150]; Sale 35 (12 August 2025) moved 10,099 bags (627,415 kg) for about KSh554 million[^151]; in July 2026, Sale 33 of the 2025/26 year averaged KSh45,419 per 50-kg bag and generated KSh884 million[^152], while Sale 40 in September averaged KSh40,931 per bag across 17,013 bags[^153]. Practitioners nonetheless describe 2025/26 as "probably the best year in four or five" — the recovery from the post-2019-floods decline — while cautioning that the spike rewards good AA volumes against declining total volume[^157]. There is a price ceiling on the specialty top: "here the best coffees go for like $5 or $10 per kilo… there's a ceiling… very difficult to find coffees here that people are willing to pay say even fifty dollars a pound," against a 2023 Best of Panama winner that auctioned at roughly $10,000/kg and an SCA Specialty Coffee Transaction Guide average near $3.70/lb[^155]. The implementation evidence for the DSS remains thinner than the statutory promise: auction reports document gross sale values and volumes but no national matched dataset linking buyer settlement date, FCS onward-payment date, and farmer net amount, and no verified count of farmers receiving cherry advances — the five-day pledge and the Coffee Act's 14-day rule should be written as legal/political deadlines, not demonstrated median payment performance[^150][^151][^153]. No source located for this update provides a consistent 2024/25 versus 2025/26 AFA production-and-acreage table or a verified unsold-lot series; those should be added only from a Coffee Directorate/AFA statistical bulletin, not back-calculated from NCE sales.

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

The specialty sector leverages both the NCE auction and the legally authorised "Second Window" direct sales pathway[^1]. High-scoring lots (85 to 90+ score AA and AB grades) command substantial premiums at auction or through direct trade contracts negotiated between international green buyers and licensed grower-marketers or cooperatives[^1]. In direct trade, the sale is operationalised through a CMA-licensed grower-marketer or cooperative broker holding legal authorisation to sign export contracts registered with the Coffee Directorate[^2]; the buyer pays via the DSS mechanism, which handles export documentation, coffee warrant transfers, and logistics through the port of Mombasa[^2].

The one-hat separation rule reshaped how specialty buyers read the catalogues: exporters can no longer "have more than one hat on your head," and named cooperative coffees (Karinga, Kagere) can only be cupped once they are milled into the auction catalogue — a delay that has pushed buyer behaviour toward in-country cupping labs[^155]. The buying season concentrates accordingly: the peak cupping and buying window runs eight to ten weeks across January and February, when "the highest-elevation and best-tasting lots arrived in the cupping labs in Nairobi"[^155][^163]. Labs are crowded: in the 2025/26 season, Norwegian, Swedish, Danish, US, Australian, and Bulgarian buyers shared the same cupping tables, with lots split between buyers such as Sweet Maria's and Melbourne Coffee Merchants/Market Lane[^158]. At the specialty margin, buyers farm the micro-lot edge: cooperative AA lots run to roughly 150 bags, single farms to 4–10, and one buyer's biological-farming project requires a 20–40-bag direct-farm minimum, bridged by an agent (Wycliffe, ex-Kahawa Bora) who connects smallholder farms to roasters[^158]. Western Kenya remains structurally neglected — exporters historically kept mills and offices near Nyeri and Nairobi, so the West never got extension services despite good coffee, and organic projects operate there — with Nairobi's barista community and tourism floated as future demand anchors[^157].

Traceability in Kenya is high relative to other smallholder origins[^1]. Coffee can be traced directly to individual FCS societies, primary washing factories (e.g., Ndaroini, Thiriku), and specific drying lots[^11]. While factory-level traceability is robust, mapping the thousands of individual smallholders who deliver cherry to a single factory presents an ongoing administrative requirement for full EUDR plot-polygon compliance[^1].

#### **5\. Actor Dynamics & Power Relationships**

In Kenya, the FCS — not the farmer — is effectively the producer of green coffee. The smallholder delivers cherry; the FCS factory owns fermentation, washing, and drying; and pooled fermentation normally erases individual farm identity, so traceability is factory-level by construction. Downstream, the chain concentrates quickly: 15 CMA-licensed brokers serve the NCE, and the five largest handle roughly 80% of exchange volumes — a mild oligopoly at the exchange gate[^53]. Exchange-side fees are capped at 1.8% of gross sale value (broker fees 1%, exchange 0.3%, DSS provider 0.3%, and CMA statutory fees 0.2%, per Legal Notice 9 of 2025), apportioned through the DSS within 24 hours of buyer funds arriving[^54].

The payment-timeline sequence should be read as a dated progression of legal duties rather than observed payouts: before the DSS, the law required payment to farmers "within seven days of bidding on a coffee"[^164]; the DSS itself settles buyer funds in roughly 48 hours[^54]; the Coffee Act requires grower proceeds within 14 days of sale (s. 74(3))[^34]; and a June 2026 presidential reform pledge promises five-day payment with at least 80% of each sale passed to the farmer[^55]. The marketing-agent era that preceded the 2023–2026 reforms illustrates how conflicts of interest compound: agents set reserve prices without owning the coffee, allegedly undersold lots, hid fees, and interfered in FCS elections to secure contracts[^52]; legal commentary on the reforms puts historical farmer retention at only ~60% of auction price[^34]. Estates bypass this entire structure through the "second window" and their own brokers, retaining the producer margin the cooperative sector was built to capture — and cooperative leaders have lobbied against direct-payment models as an existential threat, making the farmer-vs-producer boundary a live political question, not merely a definitional one.

The consolidated milling structure carries its own governance risk: a mill mix-up rejected an estate's AA+AB lot post-milling while the peaberry was fine — an offer-versus-pre-shipment divergence that underscores the per-lot fragility of the reformed system[^157]. And at the export end, the Red Sea corridor has made transit itself a quality risk: Mombasa-to-Europe shipping now runs 4–12 weeks (the 2025 journey took about 12), with containers sitting in Jeddah and Malta during heat waves, driving the switch to reefer containers[^158]. The persistent structural questions remain whether FCS pooling and cherry advances will ever match DSS exchange-speed settlement, and whether the auction's gross-price benchmarks translate into farmer net receipts once deductions, milling, brokerage, and finance are stripped out.

### **Rwanda**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Rwanda’s coffee production relies on smallholders. An estimated 400,000 smallholder households cultivate coffee across micro-plots averaging roughly 0.5 to 1 hectare (buyer accounts put the average near 1 hectare), typically containing between 200 and 300 coffee trees per farm[^4][^28]. Estate-scale coffee plantations are nearly non-existent, accounting for less than 2% of total national land planted in coffee.  
Land ownership is structured through state-administered title registration. Following national land tenure reforms, smallholders hold formal long-term land-use lease rights (*emphyteutic lease titles*) registered with the Rwanda Land Management and Use Authority (RLMUA). Land holdings are highly fragmented due to high population density and sub-division through family inheritance.  
Labour dynamics are dominated by unpaid household family labour. Tasks such as weeding, pruning, and cherry harvesting are performed by family members. During the peak harvest season (March to June), smallholders engage local day-labourers (*pise*) to pick cherries selectively. Labour costs are paid on a per-kilogram cherry picking rate. Given the micro-scale of Rwandan plots, explicit labour expenditures are low in absolute terms per household, but represent a major labour investment relative to total household land allocation.

Rwanda's smallholder structure is a colonial inheritance with a genocide-era rupture. Belgian rule restricted fertiliser use to coffee and tea, capping food crops and cattle and pushing the rural population into wage labour on commercial farms — a policy that set the sector's social shape decades before independence[^28]. The 1989 collapse of the International Coffee Agreement's quota system then halved the prices Rwandan growers received, from $1.32 to $0.77 per pound over the five years to 1994, and the genocide that followed scattered the countryside into subsistence: up to the 1990s roughly 80 per cent of Rwandans worked in agriculture, and after 1994 an estimated 90 per cent of rural households returned to subsistence farming, with the washing-station network itself rebuilt largely through aid programmes and the PEARL initiative[^28]. The density constraint is demographic — Rwanda is the most densely populated country on mainland Africa — and it shows in the farmgate: a one-hectare plot intercropped with legumes, Irish potato, gourd, and sorghum, while the highest Nyungwe-border farms above 2,200 metres receive vermiculture-fertiliser support from Tropic[^28].

Landlessness points towards mining rather than the coffee chain: artisanal coltan digging on abandoned Kivu-shore mines is the income of last resort, and Tropic frames its youth and women's programmes explicitly as keeping 'youth away from the mines and women another way to provide for their families' — third-generation grower Vincent Rukeribuga says he hopes to expand his land 'so they do not have to work mining'[^28]. Farm labour is family labour at the micro-scale: producers in Rwanda (and neighbouring Burundi) work around 400 trees and 'just do it all themselves,' with no hired crews, per a 2023 buyer's account[^73].

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary post-harvest processing in Rwanda is centralised around approximately 300 Coffee Washing Stations (CWS) distributed across key growing zones (such as Nyamasheke, Gakenke, Huye, and Rutsiro)[^3]. Smallholders pick ripe cherries and transport them on the day of harvest to local CWS[^3]. The CWS process cherries using fully washed systems (pulping, overnight fermentation, channel grading, and soaking), as well as expanding natural and anaerobic processing streams[^3][^14]. Parchment coffee is dried on raised bamboo beds under shade and full sun[^3].  
CWS ownership is split among primary farmer cooperatives, private Rwandan entrepreneurs, and multi-station corporate export entities (such as Muraho Trading Company, Baho Coffee, and Rwanda Farmers Coffee Company)[^3]. Dry milling infrastructure is centralised in and around Kigali, where dry mills hull parchment, perform mechanical size grading, density sorting, and optical defect removal, and prepare green coffee for export transport via the northern corridor (Mombasa, Kenya) or central corridor (Dar es Salaam, Tanzania).

The station tier is best read through named chains rather than the national count. COOPAC, founded in 2001 by Emmanuel Rwakagara with roughly 110 farmers, now runs six washing stations serving about 8,000 members, with agronomy and quality training, inputs, and schools, per buyer accounts[^28]. Its Kabirizi and Kigeyo stations, built roughly 300 metres above Lake Kivu, each serve about 1,000 farmers through a harvest running late February to early May, process through three-stage washing, and move cherry to bagged green in 20 to 25 days; Kirorero draws about 1,500 farmers who deliver 'primarily by boat, foot, or bicycle' — the station has no road access — and sends its processed coffee by boat to the Nyamwenda station before trucking onward; and Gishamwana operates as an island estate[^28]. Tropic's flagship Cyato station, built in 2017 in the Karundura Valley between Nyungwe National Park and Lake Kivu at roughly 1,900 metres, shows the private-operator model at full stretch: hand-sorting by colour, every lot cupped twice (once at the station and again at Tropic's Kigali lab), washed, honey, natural, and anaerobic streams, and raised-bed plus greenhouse drying[^28].

Cooperative formalisation follows a distinct arc. Abadatezuka operated informally from 2014 and formalised in 2019, joining the structure the government's 2007 codification of the 'Cooperative' had created — the step that unlocked PEARL assistance, member health insurance, loans, quality premiums, and farm inputs[^28]. Gender-differentiated marketing runs through groups such as the Gwiza Women's Group, roughly 75 members selling microlots under a gender-equity price premium[^28]. The processing economy's energy base has also shifted: the KivuWatt methane-power plant, commissioned in 2016, supplies around 30 per cent of Rwanda's electricity and is electrifying once-diesel-powered industries such as coffee processing[^28].

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

The Rwandan coffee trade operates under the regulatory oversight of the National Agricultural Export Development Board (NAEB)[^3]. The market structure experienced a structural shift in **June 2023**, when the Ministry of Agriculture and Animal Resources (MINAGRI) **repealed the Coffee Zoning Policy**[^3].  
Originally introduced in 2016, the Coffee Zoning Policy legally required smallholders to sell their cherries exclusively to designated CWS operating within their administrative district[^3]. While zoning guaranteed cherry supply to CWS and encouraged station owners to supply pre-harvest credit and inputs, it created local purchasing monopolies, suppressed farmgate cherry prices, and limited competition[^4][^22][^23].  
The 2023 repeal restored a free-market model for raw cherry purchasing[^3]. Smallholders and independent cherry aggregators (*commissionaires*) are now legally free to transport and sell cherries to any CWS offering the highest price, regardless of geographical boundary[^3]. Delivered cherries are processed at the chosen CWS, milled in Kigali dry mills, and cleared through NAEB for export. While NAEB sets a seasonal minimum cherry floor price (reported at 410 RWF/kg in 2023, 480 in 2024, and 600 in 2025, with a further reported 25% increase to 750 for the 2026 season), aggressive competition among CWS driven by repeal pushed market cherry prices past 1,000 RWF/kg in 2024[^3][^58][^36]. This price escalation significantly increased immediate cash earnings for smallholders[^3]. However, it also introduced cash flow volatility for smaller CWS and allowed informal cherry aggregators to enter the supply chain, occasionally commingling cherries from different hillsides[^3].  
The distribution of export value across nodes in the post-zoning Rwandan coffee sector is estimated as follows:

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Cherry Seller** | 65% – 75% |
| **CWS Operational Processing Costs** | 12% – 18% |
| **Dry Milling & Transit Freight (Kigali to Port)** | 8% – 10% |
| **Exporter Margin** | 5% – 8% |

Two export series bracket the post-repeal market, and the reporting periods must be kept distinct. For the 2024/25 season, Rwanda produced 21,295 tonnes and exported 20,509 tonnes, up from 16,479 tonnes in 2023/24[^165]; for fiscal-year 2024/25, coffee export revenue came to slightly more than US$116 million against US$78.7 million the prior year — a 48 per cent jump that The New Times credits with beating NAEB's 2029 export-target timeline[^166]. Calendar 2025 then set records: 23,860 tonnes of green coffee worth US$148.6 million (about RWF216 billion), volumes up 39 per cent and revenue up roughly 65 per cent year-on-year — figures that exceed the season's 20,509 tonnes precisely because the season and calendar windows are not the same[^167]. The destination mix is shifting too: Belgium, the United States, and China were the top three buyers in 2025[^168].

The cherry-price series itself has hardened. The 2025 floor of 600 RWF/kg for well-ripened cherry was confirmed as a 25 per cent increase on 2024's 480[^169], and the reported 750 for 2026 was confirmed when NAEB raised the minimum by a further 25 per cent for the 2026 season[^171]. Realised prices ran well above the floors: in 2023 the actual average paid by now-competing stations was about 650 RWF/kg against that year's 410 floor[^28], and in 2025 farmers received roughly 900 RWF/kg on average against the 600 floor[^170]. The repeal also ended a financing model: under zoning, the guaranteed supply allowed stations 'to offer pre-harvest financing and farm management support to foster quality,' and the 2023 repeal removed that guarantee at a stroke[^28]. Stations have responded politically rather than passively: on a 2023 buying trip the day's key meeting was with a sector official, the visiting station's agents 'really trying to get them to support the stations in the area and make sure that their policies for the farmers are gonna lead to some parity in terms of the other sectors' — cherry-pricing policy as a competitive lever between zones[^73]. Landlocked logistics compound the economics: shipping out of Rwanda 'is not always the easiest,' top lots rest in parchment roughly 30 days as the norm (the ideal window is 30–90), and the freshness cost is measurable — a coffee cupped two months early averaged 84 points against 86.5 for the same lot cupped later[^163].

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

Rwanda’s specialty coffee sector (80+ scoring Bourbon micro-lots, specialised natural and anaerobic fermentations) relies on single-CWS traceability and strict cherry selection protocols[^3]. The disintermediation following the repeal of the zoning policy has created operational challenges for specialty exporters[^3].  
Direct trade agreements are conducted by licensed export entities—such as specialty CWS groups or cooperative unions—holding export licenses issued by NAEB[^3]. International specialty roasters contract directly with export management to secure single-station lots (e.g., Karenge, Rugali, Bukure)[^4].  
Traceability to the specific CWS remains established[^4]. However, plot-level farm mapping required for EUDR compliance has been complicated by the repeal of zoning[^3]. Because farmers can now sell cherries across district lines to nomadic aggregators offering cash premiums, maintaining strict chain-of-custody documentation from individual farm plots to the receiving CWS requires robust digital tracking systems at reception points[^3]. Specialty exporters are deploying digital buying applications to register delivering farmers at the CWS scale in order to maintain EUDR compliance[^20].

The auction tier consolidated around the national competition. Best of Rwanda 2025 — the successor venue to Rwanda's Cup of Excellence, of which no 2025 or 2026 edition is verifiable — sold its top lot for US$88.18 per kilo at the October 2025 online auction (reportedly about fourteen times the ordinary market price per one trade account, a figure best treated cautiously) and cleared more than US$302,000 in total[^172]. Switzerland remains the largest importer of Rwandan coffee[^28], and the premium mechanics run through the same actors as the commodity tier: Gwiza's gender-equity microlots and Tropic's quality-platform model — four process streams at one flagship station, double-cupped, with youth and women's programmes — are the concrete vehicles through which quality premiums reach producers[^28].

#### **5\. Actor Dynamics & Power Relationships**

Rwandan farmers are cherry sellers at the base of a CWS-centred chain of three ownership types: farmer cooperatives (which pay second payments), private Rwandan SMEs, and multinational station chains — RTC/Westrock operates 33 washing stations and Rwacof/Sucafina 30, per buyer and industry accounts, and by some estimates six firms controlled around 70% of exports; the governance dynamics of this chain are quantified in Gerard et al.'s zoning study[^56]. Kigali dry milling is largely fee-for-service, as SMEs rarely own their own mills. CWS economics are precarious: stations require an estimated US$36 million in annual working capital sector-wide (undated TechnoServe estimate), most run below capacity, and cooperatives borrow at up to 24% versus multinationals' cheap capital. One ICO study claims a ~25% farmer margin against ~10% for the CWS, but the figure is contested and farmers are widely described as the chain's weakest link[^57].

The 2023 zoning repeal transformed farmer–CWS dynamics from monopsony to auction. Cherry floors rose from 410 RWF/kg (2023) to 480 (2024), 600 (2025), and a reported 750 (2026), while market prices exceeded 1,000 RWF/kg in 2024 — more than double that year's floor[^58]. But free-market cherry trade also unleashed inexperienced *commissionaires* who stock and resell to the highest bidder, commingling zones and breaking single-station traceability just as EUDR requires GPS-mapped, deforestation-free plots for an EU market that has historically taken the majority of exports[^168]. Pre-harvest credit (reported interest-free seasonal loans of up to 80% of crop value, such as Bank of Kigali's "IKOFI" partnership with Rwacof) persists but no longer buys loyalty: farmers now shop on price, and the long-term survival of CWS-financed credit after the repeal is contested.

EUDR formally favours Rwanda: the Commission's first benchmarking list under Regulation (EU) 2025/1093 classifies the country low-risk, entitling exporters to simplified due diligence[^84] — a position that makes the digital buying applications at station reception points a compliance complement rather than a precondition[^20]. The sector's structural priority, per World Coffee Research, is value upgrading through quality differentiation plus the rehabilitation and rejuvenation of aging trees[^173]. And the repeal's verdict is genuinely mixed: realised cherry averages of roughly 900 RWF/kg against a 600 floor suggest competitive buying does deliver farmgate gains, yet the station economics on the other side of that transfer — lost captive supply, contested pre-harvest credit, commingled traceability — remain as precarious as the working-capital arithmetic above[^170][^28].

### **Indonesia**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Indonesia’s coffee sector is predominantly smallholder-driven, with over 90% of total national output produced by smallholders operating plots averaging 1.0 hectare[^7][^8]. Commercial state-owned plantations (PTPN \- PT Perkebunan Nusantara) and large private estates account for less than 10% of national production, situated mainly in Java (growing Arabica and Robusta)[^7].  
Land tenure arrangements include formal private land titles (*Sertifikat Hak Milik* \- SHM) registered under the National Land Agency (BPN), customary land rights (*Udayat*) in Sumatran highland regions (such as Aceh Gayo, North Sumatra Lintong, and South Sumatra), and forestry margin permits[^7]. In forest border regions surrounding national parks (e.g., Bukit Barisan Selatan), smallholders cultivate coffee under state forestry co-management schemes (*Hutan Kemasyarakatan* \- HKm) or informal land encroachments[^7].  
Labour dynamics rely heavily on household family labour for daily farm operations[^7]. During the harvest season, producers engage hired local pickers (*buruh petik*) paid on a daily wage or per-*kaleng* (standard measurement tin) basis. In key Arabica regions like Aceh and North Sumatra, mutual aid labour groups (*Arisan Labor*) operate during peak picking periods. Labour costs represent 45–55% of farmgate operating expenditures, driven upward by regional rural labour constraints and competing opportunities in palm oil and mining sectors.

Indonesia's smallholder structure is a post-colonial artefact. The Dutch plantations were nationalised after the Second World War and partly subdivided for smallholders, and smallholders acquired agricultural land at a rate of roughly 8 per cent per year from 1970 onwards; today an estimated two million farming families produce about 92 per cent of the country's coffee[^29]. Robusta accounts for roughly 80–90 per cent of production depending on the data source — about 90 per cent per buyer accounts, closer to 80 per cent per World Coffee Research — and the robusta tilt is itself a rust legacy: the leaf-rust epidemics of the 1870s and 1880s drove the species' introduction[^29][^183].

Tenure's sharpest feature is state-forest occupation. Most farmers around Mount Halu in West Java 'borrow the land from the government' — Perhutani, the state forestry corporation — cultivating coffee on government-owned forest land; the yield differential punishes the arrangement, a forest tree producing roughly 200 grams per harvest against 3 to 4 kilograms from a home-garden tree, which is why Rani Mayasari of Java Halu runs a five-year campaign to move planting into home gardens[^185]. Her own holding illustrates the hybrid: 12 hectares in total, four owned and eight under Perhutani forest tenure, inside an 80-family community sharing a 60-hectare production base at about 1,350 metres[^185]. The tree stock itself is ageing: World Coffee Research estimates that nearly 70 per cent of Indonesia's coffee area needs renovation or rehabilitation, with smallholder yields low and extension fragmented[^183].

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary post-harvest processing in Indonesia is geographically distinct, dominated by the unique ***Giling Basah*** (wet hulling) technique utilised across Sumatra and Sulawesi[^7]. Under *Giling Basah*, smallholders pulp fresh cherries on-farm using small hand-cranked or gasoline-powered pulpers, ferment the pulped beans briefly in buckets or bags, wash off mucilage, and sun-dry the wet parchment for 1 to 2 days until moisture drops to 30–40% (*pergamino basah*)[^7].  
The wet parchment is sold immediately to local collectors (*Tengkulak* or *Toke*), who operate mechanical wet hullers that strip away the parchment layer while the coffee seed is still soft and moist[^7]. The hulled green-bluish beans (*labu*) are then spread on outdoor drying patios or plastic tarps by collectors and regional traders to undergo secondary drying down to the standard 12% export moisture level[^7]. In Java and Bali, conventional fully washed and natural processing methods are more common, utilising standardised CWS or cooperative processing units[^7].  
Dry milling and export preparation are concentrated in major port logistics hubs: Medan (Belawan Port) for Sumatran coffees, Surabaya (Tanjung Perak Port) for Java and Eastern Indonesian coffees, and Jakarta (Tanjung Priok Port). Central exporters operate large industrial dry mills equipped with gravity tables, size graders, and optical sorters to process the green coffee into exportable grades (e.g., Sumatra Grade 1 Triple Picked).

> **Why wet hulling?** The technique is, first and foremost, an adaptation to geography. Sumatra and Sulawesi sit on the equator with humidity routinely above 80 per cent and **two wet seasons a year** — there is no reliably dry harvest window of the kind Ethiopia or Colombia enjoy. Slow patio drying to the 11–12% export standard takes two to three weeks in that climate and invites mould, and end-of-season rain can ruin a batch outright; wet hulling instead limits the farmer's drying to one or two days at 30–40% moisture, after which the stripped bean sheds its remaining water quickly on any covered surface. Smallholder economics reinforce the adaptation: one-hectare farms cannot fund the covered dryers that would make washed processing safe, and spot-cash farmgate buyers reward speed over patience. The market then locked the profile in — the blue-green, earthy, heavy-bodied, low-acid "Sumatra style" became a specification international buyers order deliberately, and because better drying earns no premium in the commodity chain, nothing displaces wet hulling (Java and Bali, with more conventional washed processing, are the partial exception). The same collector-blending step that solves the climate problem is what commingles dozens of farms' lots, which is why Indonesia's traceability and EUDR exposure is concentrated at the aggregation layer.

Harvests follow a split calendar: Sumatra's main crop runs October to December with a March–April fly crop, while Java, Bali, Sulawesi, and Flores pick from June to August[^29]. The specialty break with wet hulling dates to around 2015, when specialty processors began deviating from the wet-hull norm toward washed and natural methods[^29]. Java Halu's 'specialty wet-hulled' protocol shows the contradiction at close range: the mill runs on order only, roughly three harvests a year, under a standardised procedure whose many steps exist 'to control the process… to control the fungi… to control the drying' — and Mayasari names the tension herself: 'if wet-hulling is supposed to be a fast, lower production time, faster turnover… but then if you're slapping specialty on top of it and now you're making it harder and more expensive, it doesn't really make sense'[^185]. The giling-basah price lock is perceptual before it is economic: 'the problem is the perception of the buyer for the giling basah price — it must be cheap,' even though 'if you want the good giling basah made by me, the price can't be cheap'[^185]. Grading power sits with the buyer, who sets ripeness bars the producer cannot contest: one buyer demanded Brix-20 naturals and discounted Brix-15 lots, asking for photos or a farm visit to verify the reading[^185]. Processing labour at Java Halu is explicitly gendered and flexible: roughly 35 women in the wet mill and 30 in the dry mill, recruited house-by-house, paid flexible hourly rates, with a weekly communal meal — a workforce that stood out at the June 2023 Fermentation Technology Camp Indonesia, where 22 of 23 tickets went to men[^185].

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

Indonesia’s internal market structure is defined by a multi-tiered collector network dominated by informal local buyers known as ***Tengkulak*** or ***Toke***[^15]. *Tengkulak* hold significant leverage over smallholders by operating as primary coffee buyers, input suppliers, and informal micro-financiers[^15]. During the non-harvest season (*paceklik*), *Tengkulak* extend credit loans and household groceries to smallholders; in return, farmers pledge their upcoming coffee harvest at discounted purchasing prices (*ijon* system)[^15].  
The chain of custody moves sequentially from the smallholder to the village *Tengkulak* (who executes the wet hulling), then to sub-district collectors, regional processing traders (who conduct secondary drying), and ultimately to large export corporation dry mills located in Medan or Surabaya before containerised export[^15][^24]. There is no central state pricing authority or mandatory coffee auction mechanism. Price discovery is governed by international NY C-Contract (Arabica) and London Robusta market fluctuations, adjusted for regional collector margin deductions.  
The share of export value captured across nodes in the Indonesian wet-hulled supply chain is structured as follows:

| Value Chain Node | Share of FOB Export Price |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Seller (Wet Parchment Value Equivalent)** | 50% – 60% |
| **Village Tengkulak / Collector (Wet Hulling & Transit)** | 15% – 20% |
| **Regional Processing Trader (Secondary Drying & Grading)** | 10% – 12% |
| **Exporter & Port Freight Logistics Hub (Medan/Surabaya)** | 8% – 10% |

The 2025–27 cycle produced an unusually wide forecasting spread, and the three USDA figures should be read as successive forecasts rather than a reconciled series. In May 2025 USDA put 2025/26 production at 11.3 million 60-kg bags and exports at 6.5 million[^174]; the December 2025 semi-annual revised production up to 12.5 million bags on improved robusta yields[^175]; and the May 2026 annual cut 2025/26 arabica to 1.37 million bags on the Aceh and North Sumatra floods while forecasting 2026/27 at about 11.38 million bags — down 8 per cent — with robusta near 10 million bags (a one-million-bag drop attributed to excessive mid-2025 rainfall disrupting flowering in southern Sumatra and Java) and green-bean exports around 7 million[^176]. Price transmission ran ahead of volumes: Indonesian bean prices rose about 25 per cent between November 2024 and March 2025[^174], and farmers held back stock from late 2024 into early 2025 anticipating further gains — the same stockholding pattern this report documents in Vietnam[^177]. Futures, not farmgate, led the move: robusta futures rallied from roughly US$3,300 per tonne on 1 August 2025 to about US$5,000 per tonne by 31 August, a 51.5 per cent rise in a month[^178]. Farm-level indications for 2025 arabica ran to Rp96,000–97,000 per kilogram, with double-picked lots near Rp105,000 — an industry indication rather than an official series[^179].

The December 2025 floods then reset the season. Rains of roughly 22–25 November across Aceh, North Sumatra, and West Sumatra left about 399,172 people displaced by 28 December per the national disaster agency — a humanitarian figure far broader than coffee — while in the coffee districts cherry collection and transport stopped outright as roads and bridges were destroyed, Aceh's main harvest was delayed into early 2026, and USDA judged that damaged trees and infrastructure could take two to three years to recover in some areas[^181][^176]. Sucafina's April 2026 update found the first half of Sumatra's arabica harvest (normally October to December) unusually small, with the March–May 2026 crop larger[^182], and relief has been routed through Asman Gayo and GARMINDO in Cafe Imports' disaster-relief campaign[^184]. For the 2026 crop, Aceh producers describe 'the lightest harvest in 15 years,' with an outside buying group inflating prices while local buyers compete for any available volume[^29].

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

The Indonesian specialty coffee sector (single-origin Gayo, Lintong, Toraja, and Java Preanger coffees scoring 80+ points) works to disintermediate the *Tengkulak* supply chain[^7]. Specialty coffee models promote fully washed, dry-hulled, or controlled experimental fermentations (anaerobic naturals) managed by smallholder farmer cooperatives (KSU \- *Koperasi Serba Usaha*) or, increasingly, by private specialty mills and estates (e.g., Asman Gayo Mill in Aceh, Frinsa in West Java, Tuang in Flores, Karana in Bali) that have led Indonesia's specialty growth more than the cooperative channel[^7][^29].  
Direct trade agreements operate under Indonesian commercial regulations via licensed green coffee exporters holding an Export Identification Number (APE); the formerly required Exporter Registered Certificate (*Eksportir Terdaftar Kopi* \- ETK) was abolished in 2021 in favour of NIB/OSS registration and ICO certificates of origin[^62]. Specialty cooperatives or direct exporters establish buying stations in producing villages, paying immediate cash premiums for fully ripe red cherries, bypassing wet hulling to produce high-grade dry-hulled or natural coffees[^7].  
Traceability presents challenges in Indonesia[^15]. The multi-tiered blending executed by *Tengkulak*, coupled with informal farming within national park borders (e.g., Bukit Barisan Selatan), creates risk regarding EUDR compliance[^7]. Achieving plot-level polygon mapping requires disintermediating the informal *Tengkulak* network and integrating smallholders into certified cooperative supply chains capable of registering farm boundaries[^15].

Indonesia's Cup of Excellence arc is short but steep: the inaugural 2022 auction sold a honey-processed Aceh lot at a top bid of US$80 per pound and cleared more than US$360,000 across 26 microlots, and the ACE programme returned in 2024 and 2026 with winning lots at 87 points and above[^180]. The named specialty mills now carry producer counts that sharpen the 2015 divergence: Asman Gayo in Central Aceh services 400 producers and has fielded multiple COE finalist lots; Karana in Kintamani, Bali, works a community of 377 smallholders; Frinsa runs an estate and dry mill in the West Java highlands; Tuang operates in Flores; and Yolan Tirta, a third-generation miller in Gowa Regency, southern Sulawesi, is establishing mini-mills and drying stations[^29]. On EUDR, Indonesia sits in the standard-risk default category — not listed in either annex of Regulation (EU) 2025/1093 — carrying full due diligence at the aggregation layer[^84], and USDA's 2025 annual explicitly flags EUDR as a factor affecting Indonesian coffee trade[^174]; the sector's existing legality-assurance schemes of the SVLK (timber) type are not sufficient substitutes for the regulation's coffee-specific geolocation and legality requirements[^63].

#### **5\. Actor Dynamics & Power Relationships**

Indonesia's farmer is only a partial producer: they pulp, ferment briefly, and skin-dry to 30–40% moisture, then sell wet parchment to a collector who performs the defining *giling basah* wet-hulling step. The classic Indonesian cup profile is therefore frequently "made" by the collector or the exporter's dry mill — not the farmer[^59]. The chain typically runs three to five tiers (village *tengkulak* → sub-district collector → regional trader → export mill in Medan, Surabaya, or Jakarta), with spot cash and quality largely unpriced: better drying earns nothing, which freezes wet hulling in place — industry estimates suggest up to 20% of value is lost to poor post-harvest practice that no actor is paid to fix.

The *ijon* system — pre-harvest sale of standing coffee at below-market fixed prices — is documented in field studies, driven by urgent cash needs, absent formal credit, and long-standing trader ties[^60]; strong social relationships lock farmers to a handful of the ~20 traders operating in a typical village[^61]. One counter-intuitive estimate from a 2018 sector quick-scan puts the farmer share near 80% of FOB for southern Sumatra robusta, attributing value loss to fragmentation and aggregation costs rather than fat intermediary margins — though multi-tier arabica chains likely return less[^59]. Export licensing liberalised with the abolition of the ETK registered-exporter requirement in 2021 (replaced by NIB/OSS registration and ICO certificates of origin)[^62], and specialty growth is led by private vertical integrators (Frinsa, Asman Arianto, Tuang, Karana) that internalise what the traditional chain fragments, paying ripeness premiums at delivery. For EUDR, plots under 4 hectares need only point locations and smartphone workflows are feasible, but plot data must be attached at the aggregation layer where collectors mix dozens of farmers' lots — and forest-margin coffee (Bukit Barisan Selatan, *HKm* permits) risks failing EUDR legality tests outright[^63].

The 2024–25 spike tested the collector chain from the farmers' side, and the result mirrors Vietnam: farmers held back stock through late 2024 into early 2025 as prices rose, out-waiting capital-constrained buyers — the same stockholding inversion documented in Vietnam — though the Indonesian evidence is thinner, since farmer-share estimates remain the dated 2018 quick-scan rather than a linked series[^177][^59]. Producer-side margin strategies are diversifying beyond coffee: Java Halu exports parchment fire-starter briquettes to Korea (from October 2023) and composts with black soldier fly larvae, monetising processing waste that the traditional chain treats as zero-value[^185]. The EUDR aggregation-layer constraint that defines the traceability problem now carries a classification: at standard risk, full due diligence applies, and plot data must still be attached where collectors mix dozens of farmers' lots — the same point where Vietnam's equivalent burden falls[^84][^63].

### **India**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

India's coffee sector is structurally polarised between a mass of very small growers and an estate sector unusually large by global standards. A Coffee Board database counted 366,242 registered coffee holdings, of which 99% are under 10 hectares; smallholders farm roughly 75% of the planted area but produce around 70% of output, while estates — a few hundred very large units, some above 100 hectares — hold about 25% of the area and generate 30% of output at higher productivity[^118]. Karnataka accounts for roughly 54% of coffee area and 69% of production (led by the Kodagu/Coorg, Chikmagalur, and Hassan hill districts), Kerala about 19% of area and 22% of output (dominated by Wayanad robusta), and Tamil Nadu most of the remainder (Nilgiris, Yercaud, Pulneys)[^119]. Non-traditional frontiers in Andhra Pradesh and Odisha (Araku Valley) and the "Seven Sister" Northeastern states host smallholder tribal coffee developed from the 1970s onward partly to wean tribal communities off shifting cultivation[^119].

Unlike the micro-plot peasant systems of East Africa, Indian coffee tenure rests on private titled land in a statutory registration system, and the estate sector operates as a genuine plantation economy: practitioner accounts describe estates carrying resident workforces on the order of "80 people working for you," housed on-estate around the owner's bungalow[^120]. Statutory welfare obligations under the Plantations Labour Act (1951) — worker housing (*labour lines*), healthcare, and schooling — have historically functioned as a de facto wage supplement tied to estate residence, though the balance between permanent and seasonal casual labour is shifting as the workforce churns. Coffee is labour-intensive even by plantation standards: an estimated 400 man-days per hectare per year for arabica and 300 for robusta, with labour around 60% of total production cost — 73% of variable cost in an irrigated-robusta costing from Chikmagalur and Hassan districts in 2017–18, at a then-wage of ₹305/man-day[^121][^118]. Labour scarcity is now structural: the children of the old resident workforce have left for Bangalore and other cities, estates increasingly depend on seasonal migrant labour, and growers respond by cutting picking rounds from two or three to one[^120][^121]. All coffee grows under a two-to-three-tier shade canopy intercropped with pepper, cardamom, arecanut, jackfruit, and citrus — a dense agroforestry system on private titled land that 300-plus years of continuous cultivation (from the legendary Baba Budan's seven smuggled seeds, planted in Chikmagalur in the 17th century) has made exceptionally biodiverse[^119][^122].

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Processing is split between farm types. Estates own their own pulperies, fermentation tanks, drying patios, and — critically in a monsoon climate — mechanical dryers; practitioner accounts describe blocks up to 65–70 degrees of slope where only the most experienced pickers can work and where a third picking round is now often strip-harvested or green-only, flowing straight into commodity grades[^115]. Small growers, lacking pulping infrastructure, overwhelmingly dry-process their robusta into sun-dried *cherry*; a minority rent pulping capacity at neighbouring estates or sell fresh cherry to estate pulpers, making the neighbouring estate — not the small grower — the de facto primary processor in much of the robusta belt[^118]. Nationally, an estimated 80% of arabica but only about 20% of robusta is wet-processed into parchment; the rest is dried as whole-cherry coffee[^121]. Because robusta cherry converts to clean coffee at roughly a 50% outturn, the small grower's headline cherry price embeds a 2:1 weight conversion the downstream buyer controls[^118].

Secondary processing is the crown jewel of the Indian system. Dry mills — universally called ***curing* works** — are concentrated in the Karnataka Malnad belt (Hassan, Chikmagalur) and around Bengaluru, and practitioner assessments rank them among the world's best, working "lot by lot, bag by bag" with Brazilian and Japanese machinery that visiting buyers find superior to much of East Africa's[^122][^123]. Curing works hull, grade, sort, bag, warehouse, and frequently finance and trade the coffee they process; many are vertically integrated into exporting, making the curer — not the farmer — the actor who determines what the export lot ultimately is[^118]. India also produces globally distinct specialty-commodity hybrids built at this stage: ***Monsooned* Malabar**, in which unwashed arabica and robusta (Cherry A/AA) are stored in Mangalore and exposed to the Southwest monsoon's humidity from June to September, swelling the beans and muting acidity; ***Kaapi* Royale**, the premium washed-robusta grade; and **Mysore Nuggets Extra Bold** arabica[^119]. Mechanisation is normalised beyond the West's romantic expectations: mechanical washers (producing *semi-wash*), mechanical dryers cycled to mimic diurnal sun, and patio-plus-machine protocols are standard on progressive estates — driven by the reality that harvest-time thunderstorms can split ripening cherries on the tree[^115][^124].

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

India is the clearest case in this report of liberalisation-by-attrition. The Coffee Board of India — a statutory body created under the Coffee Act VII of 1942 — pooled all grower coffee, fixed pool prices, and monopolised exports for half a century, a regime that stabilised small growers but froze quality incentives[^119]. Liberalisation came in steps: the 1993 Internal Sales Quota allowed 30% domestic free sale, the 1994 Free Sale Quota extended this to 70–100% for all growers, and a final amendment in September 1996 abolished pooling entirely[^119]. What persisted is gatekeeping by registration: exporters and curing works must hold Coffee Board registration, and the exporter registration (RCMC) under the Coffee Act remains the credential that makes a curing works or estate a "legitimate, buyer-facing exporter"[^125].

The post-liberalisation chain concentrates quickly downstream. Fieldwork in Chikmagalur and Hassan districts found over 90% of growers selling at farmgate to village-level traders and commission agents — "powerful local actors" who hold updated price information, extend finance, and provide storage, feeding drying yards, curing works, multinational-company agents, and exporters[^118]. Exporting is oligopolistic: the top 20 exporters control an estimated 86% of India's coffee exports[^118]. Price discovery anchors on the ICE robusta contract (London) adjusted for domestic quality premia; when global prices spiked, Coffee Board data showed arabica parchment and robusta cherry farmgate prices up 45% and 66% respectively between October 2023 and early 2024, at decade highs, with no government-held buffer to moderate the cycle[^121]. Exports hit a record 403,478 tonnes worth about $1.68 billion in calendar 2024 (+45% by value), then slipped to roughly 384,000 tonnes in 2025 even as value rose to about $2.06 billion[^126][^127], with Italy the top destination and the three largest buyers reported at a combined 51.5% of export value in 2024 — overwhelmingly green-bean shipments feeding European espresso blending[^128]. Domestic consumption is estimated by USDA at only around 1.3 million 60-kg bags (79,200 MT) for 2024/25, roughly 70% of it soluble — anchored by the cheap, chicory-blended South Indian filter-coffee tradition that producers describe as voluminous but never quality-driven[^121][^120].

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

Indian specialty coffee is estate-led rather than cooperative-led, and its institutional scaffolding is unusually state-shaped. Since the 1996 liberalisation, quality-focused estates can register as exporters and ship directly — the channel through which estates such as Kerehaklu (Chikmagalur), Ratnagiri, and Halli Berri, and platforms like Blue Tokai, market single-estate lots. Kerehaklu, a fifth-generation family estate farming 270 acres (205–210 in coffee, roughly 70% arabica / 30% robusta with boundary liberica), illustrates the model: washed robusta debuted in 2021, koji fermentations (*Aspergillus luchuensis*) reached international roasters through the Aviary label, and the farm pivoted from a nearly 100% domestic position in 2020–2022 to exporting about 55% of production to North America by 2023 — arbitrage against a domestic price ceiling while input costs climb[^115][^120]. At the institutional edge, the Coffee Board has piloted a blockchain-based marketplace and e-auction infrastructure for traceable lots, pushed Farmer Producer Organisations, and secured GIs for Bababudangiris, Coorg, Chikmagalur, and Araku Valley arabicas and Wayanad robusta (on top of the 2008 Monsooned Malabar GIs)[^118]. The Central Coffee Research Institute — which identified and disseminated cultivars including Kent, S.795, Selection 9, and Chandragiri — is a structural specialty asset, though practitioners note that its cultivars' "unromantic" numbering hamstrings origin marketing[^122][^120].

The standout non-Karnataka model is the Araku Valley (Andhra Pradesh): the Small and Marginal Tribal Farmers Mutually Aided Cooperative Society (SAMTFMACS), formed in 2007 with the Naandi Foundation and the Integrated Tribal Development Agency, aggregates more than 10,000 Adivasi farming families across 500-plus villages into the world's largest tribal coffee cooperative, marketing fully washed, terroir-mapped arabica through the Araku brand and its Paris and Bangalore flagships — "bypassing middlemen" in a supply chain that elsewhere barely reaches the curer[^129].

On **EUDR** compliance, India starts from an advantageous position mediated nonetheless by the wrong node. The EU classifies India as a low-risk country, and its two-to-three-tier shade-grown agroforestry — old-growth figs and silver oak over coffee on long-held private titled land — faces minimal deforestation exposure after the 2020 cut-off[^130]. The Coffee Board is implementing grower registration and geolocation mapping ahead of enforcement, emphasising plots above four hectares[^130]. But the compliance asymmetry tracks the sector's structural divide: estates can map their own contours, while small growers selling cherry to village traders and curing works depend on the curing-works/exporter node to carry plot-level geolocation through commingled aggregation — the same chain-of-custody bottleneck this report identifies wherever farmers sell unprocessed or semi-processed coffee[^118]. European buyers are already stockpiling low-risk shade robusta ahead of enforcement, which could turn EUDR-readiness into a de facto estate-preference filter[^131].

#### **5\. Actor Dynamics & Power Relationships**

India inverts the cherry-farmer pattern of East Africa while ending in a familiar place. The small grower typically sells sun-dried cherry or parchment — a partially finished product — to a village-level trader rather than a washing station, and the estate sector's pulperies, curing works, and export registrations make the estate-and-curer axis the de facto producer node for quality, traceability, and (now) EUDR geolocation. The Coffee Board's own costing illustrates the margin structure: in 2017–18, irrigated robusta cost about ₹89/kg to produce against a farmgate cherry price equivalent to roughly ₹129,850/ton of clean coffee (Cherry AB), while comparable Belgian export values ran about ₹164,000/ton — a spread of ₹34,147/ton, roughly a fifth of exporter-level value, absorbed between farmgate and port[^118]. The lever is the familiar one: village-level traders capture business through liquidity — finance, storage, instant cash at the gate — and 90% of growers sell at the farmgate because cash-on-delivery beats financing one's own way to a curing works[^118]. The Indian counterpart to this report's *acopiador* and *tengkulak* is the village consolidator and the commission agent working for curing works and exporters.

Payment timing is likewise the farmgate's decisive lever, but in India it operates through the estate's payroll as much as the trader's wallet: estates pay resident and casual pickers through the season, effectively financing the harvest internally, while small growers selling to traders face spot-cash discounts against waiting for curing-works or exporter terms. Producer-side power is thinning at the edges — labour shortage is compressing picking rounds into strip harvests that feed the commodity stream[^115], and the domestic specialty price ceiling pushed flagship estates to export the majority of their crop[^120]. The counter-current is institutional: Coffee Board FPO promotion, the blockchain marketplace pilot, and GI branding all aim to move small growers up the chain[^118], and Araku shows that aggregation can compress intermediation entirely when a processing-capable institution holds the quality infrastructure[^129]. But the structural reading matches this report's second thesis: whoever finishes the processing — the estate pulpery, the curing works with its screens and optical sorters, the exporter holding the registration — remains the node that quality premiums, traceability data, and EUDR polygons must pass through, and in India that node is not the 99%-smallholder farmgate.

### **Vietnam**

#### **1\. Farm Ownership & Land Tenure Structures**

Vietnam is the world's second-largest coffee producer and its dominant robusta exporter, shipping an estimated 28–31 million bags of green coffee across 2023–25 marketing years against roughly 1 million bags of arabica[^132][^133]. Robusta is approximately 97% of national output[^134], and Vietnam alone supplies over 40% of the world's robusta — more than double the shares of second-placed Brazil (≈25%) and Indonesia (≈13%)[^135]. The position was built in a generation: *Đổi mới* reforms (1986) reopened private farming, and state-sponsored migration brought an estimated four to five million mostly Kinh settlers into the Central Highlands after 1975; World Coffee Research estimates robusta output rose some 2,100% between 1990 and 2024[^136][^134]. Total cultivated area stood at roughly 730,000 hectares in 2024, with the five Central Highlands provinces — Đắk Lắk (the traditional core around Buôn Ma Thuột), Lâm Đồng, Đắk Nông, Gia Lai, and Kon Tum — accounting for 92% of area and 90% of output[^133]. Arabica survives only in high-elevation pockets — most prominently the Đà Lạt (Lâm Đồng) highlands, including Cầu Đất, and Sơn La in the northwest — alongside a historically significant *excelsa* minority (*Coffea dewevrei*, locally *cà phê mit*, "jackfruit coffee") in Quảng Trị that official statistics barely register[^137][^138]. Production is overwhelmingly smallholder: a World Bank–funded study counted roughly half a million smallholder farmers in Đắk Lắk province alone, and industry estimates commonly put the national household count at 1.2–1.4 million with average plots near half a hectare[^134][^133].

All agricultural land remains state-owned: households hold long-term, transferable land-use-right certificates — the *sổ đỏ*, or "red book" — rather than freehold title, a system rooted in the post-1975 collectivisation of agriculture and the *Đổi mới* reallocation of use rights[^134]. Tenure documentation is sharply unequal because the state engineered the land allocation: Kinh settlers generally obtained certificates, whereas indigenous Ede and Jarai (Montagnard) households on ancestral plots frequently did not — and Đắk Lắk household studies found Ede farmers holding larger coffee farms yet earning less than their Kinh neighbours, a gap attributed to unequal access to labour, credit, and extension[^134]. Farmer and media accounts describe durian and other high-value perennials displacing robusta, although official statistics still show net coffee expansion[^133]. Aging tree stock is the more quantified constraint — a 2011 World Bank estimate warned that without replanting, production could fall 30–40% by the mid-2020s — and replanting has since leaned on institutes such as WASI (Western Highland Agriculture and Forest Science Institute), supplying an estimated 4–5 million seedlings a year; drought meanwhile cut projected 2025 output by about 2% and put Đắk Lắk's irrigation under scrutiny[^134][^133].

During the November–January harvest, families do most maintenance and early picking, supplemented by hired pickers paid per kilogram of cherry. Practitioner accounts from 2023 describe an emerging labour crunch — around Đà Lạt, the cut-flower industry pays pickers nearly double what coffee farmers can offer — while an ageing farming population faces a crop demanding a ten-year investment horizon; producer commentary increasingly argues that agroforestry, not further volume, is the long-run equilibrium for Vietnamese coffee plots[^132].

#### **2\. Processing Infrastructure & Milling Dynamics**

Primary post-harvest processing is decentralised but unusually shallow. The commodity norm is natural processing: farmers harvest — historically by strip-picking whole branches for volume, a practice practitioner accounts credit with building the origin's low-quality reputation — then sun-dry whole cherries on patios or sell fresh cherry the same day to village collectors[^132]. Mechanical dryers cover the shortfall where patio space or end-of-season rains fail, and the farmer's dried product — *cà phê xô* (dried cherry) or dried bean ("*nhân xô*") — moves through village and district collectors (*đại lý*) who perform mechanical hulling and polishing, followed by exporter dry mills that screen-grade (robusta Grades 1–3), density-sort, and optical-sort for container loading[^133]. Structurally, the Vietnamese farmer finishes the drying step that sets commodity quality — more than Indonesia's *tengkulak*-dominated chain ever gives the farmer — but the lot's commercial identity is manufactured by the *đại lý* and the exporter's mill, which blend hundreds of farmgate deliveries into standardised export grades[^133].

Quality upgrading is a collector- and exporter-level activity. Washed arabica is processed in Lâm Đồng and Sơn La, while the frontier of robusta improvement sits almost entirely with private processors: honey-processed robusta (robusta pulp carries more sugar than arabica, and Vietnam's comparatively mild robusta profile rewards the added sweetness) and anaerobic or carbonic maceration fermentations, which tame harshness and solve robusta's mucilage-removal problem[^82][^139]. Downstream, investment is shifting from raw-bean volume to processing capacity — Nestlé, Trung Nguyên, and Highlands Coffee are all expanding — while processed (soluble and roasted) exports rose from 8.8% of volume in 2022 to 9.6% in 2024, still leaving roughly 90% of exports as green bean[^133].

#### **3\. Market Structure, Power Dynamics & Selling Channels**

Vietnam's internal market has no auction, no state price floor, and no institutional price-stabilisation body. Price discovery runs off the ICE Robusta ("London") futures curve plus a domestic basis quoted daily in đồng per kilogram of dried bean; the Vietnam Coffee–Cocoa Association (VICOFA) plays a coordinating and trade-promotion role rather than a regulatory one[^133]. The chain of custody is short — typically farmgate seller → village *đại lý* / provincial collector → exporter dry mill — but export capacity is concentrated among a small group of large firms (USDA names Simexco Đắk Lắk and EDE among the major exporters), and the sector's fragility was demonstrated in the 2013–14 price bust, when half of some 127 export firms ceased trading under about 8 trillion Đồng (US$379 million, ~60% of sector lending) in non-performing loans[^133][^134]. Green bean buyers are dominated by the EU (41% of exports, led by Germany at an average US$3,390/tonne in 2023–24), with a fast-growing Asian premium tier — the Philippines paid the highest average unit value, US$4,424/tonne, in 2023–24[^133]. When an origin under-supplies, buyers switch — trade-education accounts describe roasters moving to "green coffee for $2 per pound from Honduras or Vietnam" — because farmers at the bottom of the chain do not set prices[^140].

The 2023–25 robusta price surge restructured bargaining power in ways that directly illustrate the payment-timing lever. Average export prices reached US$5,630/tonne in the first half of 2024/25 — up 143 per cent versus the same period of the prior marketing year (the full 2023–24 crop averaged US$3,792/tonne) — while domestic farmgate prices reached roughly VND 125,000/kg — 130% above the prior year, against a VND 40,000–50,000/kg era two seasons earlier[^133]. As prices rose, farmers withheld stocks in anticipation of further gains, forcing exporters into a just-in-time, buy-to-sell model because procuring inventory at VND 100,000+/kg had become too capital-intensive; first-half 2024/25 export volumes consequently fell about 23% despite record prices[^133]. Payment at the farmgate remains cash-on-delivery and procurement margins are thin — which is precisely why the collector's liquidity, not any quality differential, is the binding constraint in most seasons.

| :---- | :---- |
| **Farmgate Seller (Dried Cherry / Green-Bean Equivalent)** | 70% – 80% |
| **Village Collector / Primary Processor (*đại lý*)** | 5% – 8% |
| **Exporter Dry Milling, Grading & Warehousing** | 6% – 10% |

#### **4\. Specialty Coffee Value Chain Nuances**

The Vietnamese specialty sector is being built inside a closed-loop domestic market — the only major origin where the country's best coffees may never be exported. Will Frith, the consultant and roaster widely treated as the scene's central figure (Re:Co talk "Vietnam, a new vocabulary"; roastery Building Coffee opened 2019 with partner Kel, joined in 2021 by Ed Leebrick of Lighthouse Roasters; café BEL 2021), argues that specialty must add to Vietnam's entrenched coffee culture rather than replace it, and that domestic demand sustains a genuinely closed specialty loop: the best Vietnamese coffee still stays in Vietnam[^132][^138]. His motto — "better coffee for more people" — has become shorthand for the scene's inclusive rather than micro-lot-exclusive strategy[^141]. Practitioner accounts from 2023 confirm the resulting price inversion: specialty-grade arabica grown less than a six-hour drive from Sài Gòn sells better domestically (specialty cups US$2.50–10 against a US$1 street iced milk coffee) than on export markets — a reversal of the export-drain pattern seen at every other origin in this report, with even Latin American micro-lots now finding their first Asian buyers in Vietnam[^132][^142].

Institutionally, the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development's 2021 specialty coffee programme targets 19,000 specialty hectares (3% of coffee area) and 11,000 tons of output by 2030, with VICOFA running specialty competitions for international buyers[^133]. Processing experiments follow global specialty practice — honey-processed robusta, anaerobic fermentations — adapted to robusta's higher pulp sugar load, but premium robusta still trades only at "C-market-plus" prices well below specialty arabica levels: Nguyen Coffee Supply (founded 2018) is the first US specialty importer-roaster focused on Vietnamese robusta, and World Coffee Research is preparing its first robusta breeding programme[^82][^136]. The Saigon café-roastery 96B has meanwhile worked *excelsa* from Hướng Phùng (Quảng Trị) since 2021 — ladders for the 12-metre trees, thick mucilage to manage — after the August 2025 reclassification restored *Coffea dewevrei* as a species in its own right[^137].

EUDR compliance is the sector's binding external constraint. The EU takes roughly 41% of Vietnam's coffee exports, and the EU Deforestation Regulation — in force for coffee in 2026 — requires geolocated, deforestation-free plots with a December 2020 cut-off[^133][^143]. Vietnam's land-conversion history makes this non-trivial: Central Highlands coffee expansion is documented to have displaced both forest and shifting cultivation, and practitioner accounts of the robusta belt describe deforestation pressure connected to land grabbing[^134][^143]. The compliance architecture is accordingly being built at the exporter and state level: Simexco Đắk Lắk has implemented lot-level traceability and EDE has completed EUDR-compliant exports to the EU, while MARD's regional monitoring systems are collecting data on 136,000 hectares of coffee land, flagging high-risk "coffee grown within forest" areas and standardising data for certification programmes[^133]. As in Indonesia, the polygon-mapping burden for over a million half-hectare households falls at the aggregation layer where *đại lý* blend deliveries — so compliance capacity accrues to exporters first, reaching unorganised farmers only through them[^133].

#### **5\. Actor Dynamics & Power Relationships**

Vietnam's farmgate seller is a partial producer — more so than Indonesia's, less so than Colombia's. The farmer harvests and sun-dries the whole cherry — the step that makes commodity robusta shippable — but the *đại lý* and exporter mill own hulling, polishing, grading, and lot identity; the seller absorbs moisture and defect discounts set entirely by the buyer's scale and screens, and a farm's coffee enters a container indistinguishable from its neighbours'[^132][^133]. The "race to the bottom" that built the origin's reputation was a rational response to a market that priced volume, not quality; practitioners now frame the sector's task as shifting from *terroir* romanticism to *provenance*, the record of ownership that follows a coffee from farmer through processor to roaster[^132].

The payment-timing lever cuts both ways here: the chain runs on cash-on-delivery at the farmgate, with exporters and traders holding the working capital — the liquidity-for-access mechanism documented from Colombia's *acopiadores* to Indonesia's *tengkulak*[^133]. But the 2024–25 price spike inverted the usual asymmetry: farmers holding physical stock and facing no urgent debt service out-waited exporters whose procurement had become capital-intensive, cutting first-half export volumes by roughly 23% — evidence that when farmgate liquidity is not the binding constraint, farmer bargaining power can dominate the domestic basis[^133]. Specialty practice adds a second counter-lever: Frith's argument that the "most consistent bidder" matters more than the "highest bidder" reframes the buyer's value as reliability rather than price, and the closed-loop market means the competitive check on collectors and exporters now increasingly comes from domestic roasters and café chains bidding for the same arabica at prices the export market would not pay — a better price "here" than at export, an upside-down pattern unique among this report's origins[^132].

Downstream concentration and regulation will define the next phase: procurement capital-intensity and EUDR traceability both favour large, capitalised exporters, and the 2013–14 insolvency wave — 60% of sector loans at risk — shows how quickly a price reversal wipes out thinly capitalised buyers, an echo of the 1997 crash triggered partly by Vietnamese and Brazilian volume surges[^134][^26]. The plausible equilibrium is two-track: a high-volume stream in which capitalised exporters such as Simexco Đắk Lắk and EDE internalise milling, traceability, and compliance while farmgate capture stays structurally high, and a small specialty stream in which premiums flow only through the processors who control fermentation, drying, and polygon mapping — re-confirming this report's second thesis that the processing node, not the farmer, is the effective counterparty for quality, traceability, and EUDR compliance in Vietnam[^133][^139].

> **"C plus differential": how origin prices are set:** almost no farmgate price in this report is set locally. The reference is a futures contract — the ICE New York "C" for arabica, London robusta for the robusta belt — and origin prices are quoted as that reference plus (or minus) a **differential**: a premium or discount reflecting the origin's quality, availability, freight, and risk[^103]. Vietnam runs a domestic *basis* off London, quoted daily in đồng per kilogram of dried bean; Colombia's FNC formula is literally the C settlement price × exchange rate + a Colombian differential; Brazil's internal *caixa* benchmarks track the same contract. The differential is where competition between origins — and between buyers within an origin — is actually fought. The system also carries a systemic risk the 2024–25 spike exposed: a trader who sells forward at a fixed differential must still deliver when the underlying futures price doubles, and futures positions are settled in cash daily — the margin calls that consumed an estimated $7 billion in a single month and pushed Atlantica and CafeBras into court-led restructuring[^105][^106][^107]. Growers holding physical stock were on the winning side of that trade[^133][^107].

## **Strategic Conclusions and Policy Implications**

This structural analysis reveals that global coffee supply chains are experiencing institutional reconfigurations driven by legal reforms, quality differentiation, and global regulatory mandates. Across the ten analysed origins, distinct policy trajectories and structural challenges emerge:  
The deregulation of historical trading monopolies—evidenced by Ethiopia’s Directive No. 433/2019 and Rwanda’s 2023 repeal of its Coffee Zoning Policy—demonstrates a global shift towards direct market integration[^3]. While these measures increase immediate farmgate cherry prices by fostering competition among buyers, they can also disrupt primary processing infrastructure, incentivise opportunistic short-term cherry trade, and complicate plot-level chain-of-custody tracking[^3].  
Kenya’s Direct Settlement System (DSS) under the Capital Markets Authority represents an institutional framework for eliminating payment delays and restricting excessive administrative fees within smallholder cooperative structures[^2]. Automating funds disbursement directly from central clearing exchanges to producer accounts reduces predatory intermediary leverage and speeds settlement, though farmgate liquidity still depends on cooperative pooling practices and the timing of first payments[^30].  
In origins lacking institutional price floors or direct cooperative outreach—such as Indonesia’s *Tengkulak* network and Peru’s unorganised *Acopiador* stream—smallholders remain vulnerable to informal credit locks and misgraded purchasing deductions[^5]. Strengthening producer cooperatives and expanding local micro-finance access are essential preconditions for improving farmgate margin retention.  
Finally, the implementation of the European Union Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) presents an operational challenge across smallholder-dominated origins. Nations with centralised coffee databases and established cooperative mapping (such as Colombia’s SICA) are relatively well-positioned to comply[^1]. Conversely, origins characterised by micro-plot garden systems, informal forestry border cultivation, and multi-tiered collector blending (e.g., Indonesia, Ethiopia, post-zoning Rwanda) face potential supply chain disruptions unless significant resources are directed towards plot-level polygon mapping and digital traceability systems[^3]. Securing the long-term economic sustainability of the specialty coffee value chain requires balancing price transparency with institutional support for primary processing infrastructure, ensuring that quality-tier premiums flow transparently through to individual producers at the farmgate level[^2].

Several origins sharpen both cross-cutting theses by supplying systematic exceptions and confirmations. Brazil confirms the processing-ownership thesis from the opposite direction: because the farm owns wet milling, dry milling, and often export, farmgate capture is the highest in this report and EUDR polygons are a farm's own administrative record (the CAR registry) — but the same inversion relocates the coercive frontier downstream, to piece-rate picking labour and the documented slavery-like conditions that persist on some plantations[^95][^96][^97]. Vietnam shows the payment-timing lever reversing under scarcity: when farmers hold stocks and face no urgent debt service, exporters' procurement capital-intensity becomes the vulnerability, and first-half 2024/25 export volumes fell roughly 23% despite record prices[^133]. India shows that dismantling a state marketing board (1996) leaves durable gatekeeping in registration and curing works rather than returning power to the farmgate, with the top 20 exporters controlling an estimated 86% of exports[^118][^122]. Guatemala shows how price collapse and leaf rust can convert a coffee economy into a labour-export economy — the 2019 C-price collapse fed the very out-migration that now thins its harvest workforce — and how institutional polygon mapping (ANACAFÉ's ANATEC/GIS, the Enveritas partnership) can outrun smallholder income capture[^86][^87][^85].

Across origins, two actor-level dynamics cut through every market structure analysed above. First, payment timing is the decisive lever of farmgate power: actors who convert liquidity into market access — Colombia's *acopiadores*, Ethiopia's *akrabis*, Indonesia's *Tengkulak*, Kenya's FCS factories through cherry advances, Guatemala's *compradores*, and India's village-level traders — consistently outcompete higher-paying but slower institutions such as cooperatives and state price floors, with Brazil as the systematic exception (liquidity sits with the growers themselves, and the 2024–25 margin-call distress hit traders rather than farmers) and Vietnam as the conditional exception when stockholding farmers out-wait capital-constrained exporters. Interventions that raise headline prices without addressing payment timing (DSS-style settlement automation, cooperative dividend systems) therefore risk being undercut in practice. Second, the actor who controls post-harvest processing — not the farmer — is usually the effective counterparty for quality, traceability, and EUDR compliance: the FCS factory in Kenya, the CWS in Rwanda and Ethiopia, the *Tengkulak* collector in Indonesia, India's estate-and-*curing*-works axis, Vietnam's collector–exporter mill, versus the integrated farmer-producer in Colombia and Peru and, at estate scale, in Brazil. Policy and direct-trade programmes should be designed around that processing node, since premiums, polygon-mapping requirements, and quality incentives reach the farmgate only if they pass through — or are contractually tied to — the actor who actually processes the coffee.
